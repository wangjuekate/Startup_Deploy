Financial Budgeting

MENT 3235 · Module 8 · Concept Lecture · Tuesday, November 10

Case: Marvell FY2026 · Mental model: the Reversibility Budget

A budget is not a forecast. It is a set of promises, and they are not equally easy to break. Open with the hands — the laugh when they drop is the setup for the whole hour.

A budget is a work of fiction

  • Show of hands: who has ever made a budget? (most hands)
  • Keep it up if you stuck to it. (hands drop)
  • So we all know a budget is fiction. What I want to convince you of is that most of it is fiction and some of it absolutely is not — and the entire skill is knowing which lines are which.

Two activities, one word

Some of what you write down is an estimate: we think we will spend about this much on marketing.

Some of it is a contract: we signed a piece of paper promising to pay this, and it will be paid whether or not the year goes the way we said.

Estimates flex. Contracts do not. A budget that does not tell you which is which is not a plan. It is a wish with columns.

Let the laugh land before the box. The distinction between an estimate and a contract is the entire lecture, stated in the first ninety seconds.

The plane that broke the airline

  • Airlines order aircraft years before they fly them. You get in a queue, you put money down, you take delivery in three or four years — and you were required to guess in advance what travel demand would look like when the plane arrives.
  • Imagine you placed that order in 2019. You forecast a great few years. You committed.
  • And then in 2020 nobody flew anywhere at all.

The asymmetry that actually hurt

The demand forecast was wrong — fine, everybody's was. But the forecast was revised in a morning and the commitment took years to unwind.

The planes were still coming. The deposits were still spent. Airlines spent the entire crisis negotiating to delay deliveries, because the one thing they could not do was un-sign.

Rule one: you make commitments based on a forecast, and you pay them based on reality.

Any long-lead industry works here if the room is not interested in aircraft — foundry capacity is the one they will meet on Thursday.

Not all money is equally spent

Committed

Signed. Contractual. Multi-year supply agreements, capacity reservations, leases, debt service, deposits already paid. You can sometimes get out — cancellation fees, forfeited deposits, or losing a place in a queue you waited years to join. Time to stop: months to never.

Sticky

Not contractual, but not fast either. People, mostly. Engineering programs halfway to a design win. A factory ramp. You can stop these — you just destroy something on the way out, and rebuilding takes as long as building did. Time to stop: a quarter or two, with damage.

Flexible

Discretionary. Marketing campaigns, travel, contractors, buybacks, optional projects. Turn it off Monday, turn it back on in March. Time to stop: immediately.

When the year goes badly, all the pressure falls on the third bucket — because it is the only one that moves. Which means the thing that gets cut is not the thing that should be cut. It is the thing that can be. And it is usually the smallest bucket, and frequently the most valuable per dollar.

Slow down. This is the actual content. One question defines the bucket: how fast could I stop this, and what would stopping cost?

Budget by reversibility, not by percentage

What most people do

Budget by percentage of revenue: R&D should be 20%, marketing 10%, that is what our industry does.

Fast, and roughly harmless — until revenue misses, at which point the percentages tell you nothing about what to do.

What to do instead

How much of next year is already decided? Add up the committed bucket. That is what you spend no matter what happens — compare it to your worst plausible revenue, not your expected revenue.

If the floor is higher, you have not written a budget. You have written a bet — which may be the right call, but it should be a decision, not a discovery.

And then the question that saves companies: how much of my flexibility have I already spent? Every commitment you sign converts flexible money into committed money, permanently, for its term. Optionality is a resource. You can run out of it.

The worst-plausible-revenue comparison is the exercise. Make them do it for their own venture in two minutes; most have never written the floor down.

The mental model — the Reversibility Budget

The number at the bottom matters less than the shape above it — and the shape is how much of this can I still change?

Beat 4 is the one that changes behaviour: the cut lands where it can, not where it should. Beat 5 is the sentence Thursday turns on.

How to use it — four steps

1 · Sort every line

By one question only: how long would it take to stop this, and what would stopping cost?

2 · Total the committed

For next year. That is your floor — the spending that happens whatever the revenue does.

3 · Compare it to the worst case

Not to expected revenue. If the floor is higher, you are running a bet.

4 · Look at what flexible is left

That is your entire ability to respond to being wrong. If it is thin, you have already spent your optionality.

Step 3 is the one nobody does. Make them say the worst plausible number out loud before they see the committed total.

The asymmetries that make it a model

Forecast versus fact

Commitments are made on a forecast and paid on the actual.

Every multi-year supply agreement is a bet on a demand number somebody typed into a spreadsheet. The number gets revised quarterly. The agreement does not.

And where the real damage happens

Demand can cancel on you faster than supply can cancel on you.

Customers buy on purchase orders they can pull with short notice. Suppliers sell on contracts you signed for years. So when demand turns, your revenue falls immediately and your costs fall slowly — and the gap between those two speeds is exactly the size of the crisis.

The second box is the case in advance. Write the two speeds on the board and leave them up through Thursday.

The one question the model makes you ask

Not “can we afford this?” — but if the demand we are assuming does not arrive, how long does it take to stop paying for it — and what does stopping cost?

A budget is not a prediction of the future. It is a record of how much of the future you have already promised away.

Every team has one line they cannot answer this about. Finding it is the point of the exercise, not the number they eventually give.

Your job

Not to admire the growth. To decide — and to know which of your decisions you could take back.
