Stage 1 — Key takeaways

Price the commitments, then allocate

Financial Budgeting: Budget by how quickly spending can stop.

  • Combine the private evidence. The CFO sets the reserve and the ceiling; the COO prices staggering; the CMO prices customer widening; the CTO explains the engineering choices.
  • Calculate the real cost. Staggering (40) and widening (25) leave 35 of the 100 planning units. Integration costs 30 × 1.5 = 45 because it adds committed payments through fiscal 2029.
  • Respect the reserve. Cash covering the $1,871.8 million of fiscal 2027 foundry commitments comes first. After staggering, 60 planning units remain; divestiture cash adds nothing beyond the reserve.

Stage 2 — Make Executive Decisions

Chief Financial Officer (CFO)

The automotive ethernet sale brought in $2.5 billion in cash. The company holds about $2.6 billion of cash, owes $4.5 billion of borrowings with $499.8 million due within twelve months, has an undrawn $1.5 billion revolver, pays a quarterly dividend and repurchases stock. It owes $1,871.8 million to foundries in fiscal 2027 and has just spent about $1.6 billion of cash on Celestial AI and XConn. Where does the money go?

  • A.Fund the acquisitions and the contingent payments. Celestial and XConn cost about $1.6 billion in cash, and Celestial may require more cash and shares through fiscal 2029.
  • B.Retire debt. $4.5 billion is outstanding with $499.8 million due within twelve months, and interest expense was $202.6 million — permanently reducing the fixed charge lowers the floor every future year has to clear.
  • C.Hold enough cash against the $1,871.8 million of foundry commitments due in fiscal 2027. It is the least reversible obligation on the books and it gets paid whether or not the demand behind it shows up.
  • D.Return it to shareholders through buybacks and dividends. We grew 42%, earned a 32.6% net margin, and the balance sheet has never looked stronger.

Stage 2 — Make Executive Decisions

Chief Marketing Officer (CMO)

Distributor A was 37% of net revenue, up from 34% and 24%. Direct Customer A was 14%. Four customers were 73% of gross accounts receivable, and 77% of revenue shipped to customers with operations in Asia. U.S. export restrictions on certain Chinese customers are expected to keep affecting revenue, and some customers “amass large inventories of our products well in advance of need.” Where does the commercial effort go?

  • A.Into securing longer, firmer commitments from the largest customers, so the demand side of the ladder starts to look like the supply side.
  • B.Into supporting the customers building inventory ahead of export restrictions. They are buying now, they are buying a lot, and revenue booked is revenue booked.
  • C.Into the data center market as hard as possible. It grew 46% and that is where the AI demand behind every one of these commitments actually is.
  • D.Into widening the customer base. Our costs are committed for years and our revenue arrives on purchase orders that can be cancelled with short notice — and one distributor has gone from 24% to 37% of revenue in two years.

Stage 2 — Make Executive Decisions

Chief Operating Officer (COO)

Unconditional purchase commitments to foundries and test and assembly partners total $2,665.8 million — $1,871.8 million of it in fiscal 2027, $476.6 million in fiscal 2028 and under $70 million a year after that. Capacity reservation agreements run 4 to 10 years, with at least $458.2 million of purchase levels through fiscal 2033. Cancellation “requires payment of all costs and expenses incurred through the date of cancellation” and may mean “loss of priority to reserved capacity.” What do you do with the ladder?

  • A.Diversify where it is made. Most qualified foundries sit in one region of Taiwan and assembly and test in the Pacific Rim — the concentration risk is geographic before it is financial.
  • B.Re-shape the ladder so commitments are staggered across years instead of stacked. $1,871.8 million of the $2,665.8 million total falling in one year is a single bet on a single demand forecast.
  • C.Commit more capacity now while it is available. AI demand is here, data center revenue grew 46%, and reserved capacity is the constraint on how much of it we can serve.
  • D.Negotiate the exit terms rather than the volumes. If cancellation costs and loss of queue priority are the real penalty, that is what should be renegotiated.

Stage 2 — Make Executive Decisions

Chief Technology Officer (CTO)

Research and development is $2,075.2 million — 25.3% of revenue, down from 33.9% because revenue grew faster than spending, not because spending fell. $409.0 million of it is stock-based compensation. The company just bought Celestial AI (about $1.3 billion in cash plus 24.5 million shares, with contingent payments through fiscal 2029) and XConn ($280 million plus 2.1 million shares). How do you budget engineering?

  • A.Stage it against committed design wins. R&D is the stickiest money we have — stopping a programme mid-flight loses the socket and the team — so each stage should be tied to a customer commitment that already exists.
  • B.Hold the R&D dollar amount flat and let it fall as a share of revenue. It has already gone from 33.9% to 25.3% and the business grew anyway.
  • C.Spend up to keep the ratio where the industry expects it. If revenue is $8.2 billion, R&D should scale with it, and cutting the ratio signals retreat.
  • D.Concentrate on integrating what we just bought. Celestial and XConn cost about $1.6 billion plus contingent payments through fiscal 2029, and unintegrated acquisitions are the most expensive shelf-ware there is.

Stage 2 — Key takeaways

Fund flexibility around the fixed obligations

Financial Budgeting: Connect the payment schedule to demand uncertainty.

  • Protect the committed bucket. Reserve cash for the fiscal 2027 foundry bill. The 32.6% net margin includes a $1.8 billion one-time divestiture gain; it does not establish recurring cash available to spend.
  • Fund one complete plan. Stagger the ladder (40) + widen the customer base (25) + stage R&D against committed design wins (35) = 100 units after the reserve.
  • Compare the opportunity cost. Replacing design-win staging with integration makes the plan cost 110. Widening supports the staggered ladder, while staged R&D releases sticky spending against customer commitments.

Stage 3 — Key takeaways

Rank the obligations that survive a downturn

Financial Budgeting: Test the budget against worst-case revenue.

  • Put Commitments and Money first. Either order fits the template: the foundry payments remain due even if AI demand falls short, and cash must cover those obligations.
  • Place Concentration third and Engineering fourth. Customers can cancel faster than the company can stop paying. Widening reduces dependence on one distributor; staging already ties engineering to design wins.
  • Name the bucket and test the downside. Use evidence from two executives. Identify COMMITTED, STICKY or FLEXIBLE spending, then explain what still has to be paid if expected revenue does not arrive.
