Marvell's Commitment Ladder

Simulation Reflection · Thursday, November 12

Group Discussion

  • Form groups with classmates who have the same role.
  • As a group, name the bucket your decision sits in — COMMITTED, STICKY or FLEXIBLE — and how long it takes to stop.
  • Cite a figure from your packet, name what your choice gives up, and state one thing that would change your answer.
  • Select one representative to present your group's choice and reasoning.
💰

Financial Lead

Where does the money go?

  • A Fund the acquisitions and the contingent payments. Celestial and XConn cost about $1.6 billion in cash, and Celestial may require more cash and shares through fiscal 2029.
  • B Retire debt. $4.5 billion is outstanding with $499.8 million due within twelve months, and interest expense was $202.6 million — permanently reducing the fixed charge lowers the floor every future year has to clear.
  • C Hold enough cash against the $1,871.8 million of foundry commitments due in fiscal 2027. It is the least reversible obligation on the books and it gets paid whether or not the demand behind it shows up.
  • D Return it to shareholders through buybacks and dividends. We grew 42%, earned a 32.6% net margin, and the balance sheet has never looked stronger.
💰

Financial Lead — Key Takeaways

  • Cash allocation changes future flexibility. Explain which obligations the proposal meets and which choices remain available afterward.
  • Compare sources with uses over time. Distinguish recurring operations, one-time proceeds and payments falling due when assessing the plan.
  • Test a demand shortfall. State what happens to available cash and required payments if the expected revenue arrives late or does not arrive.

Marketing Lead

One distributor is 37% of revenue, and everything behind it is committed for years. Where does the commercial effort go?

  • A Into securing longer, firmer commitments from the largest customers, so the demand side of the ladder starts to look like the supply side.
  • B Into widening the customer base. Our costs are committed for years and our revenue arrives on purchase orders that can be cancelled with short notice — and one distributor has gone from 24% to 37% of revenue in two years.
  • C Into supporting the customers building inventory ahead of export restrictions. They are buying now, they are buying a lot, and revenue booked is revenue booked.
  • D Into the data center market as hard as possible. It grew 46% and that is where the AI demand behind every one of these commitments actually is.
📣

Marketing Lead — Key Takeaways

  • Commercial choices shape the demand side of the budget. Explain how the proposal changes the timing, certainty and concentration of customer orders.
  • Compare the two cancellation clocks. Assess how quickly demand can change relative to the spending needed to supply it.
  • Test the evidence behind the forecast. Identify what would distinguish durable demand from temporary ordering behavior and how the budget would respond.
⚙️

Operation Lead

Seventy percent of every commitment you have falls in one year. What do you do with the ladder?

  • A Diversify where it is made. Most qualified foundries sit in one region of Taiwan and assembly and test in the Pacific Rim — the concentration risk is geographic before it is financial.
  • B Re-shape the ladder so commitments are staggered across years instead of stacked. $1,871.8 million of the $2,665.8 million total falling in one year is a single bet on a single demand forecast.
  • C Negotiate the exit terms rather than the volumes. If cancellation costs and loss of queue priority are the real penalty, that is what should be renegotiated.
  • D Commit more capacity now while it is available. AI demand is here, data center revenue grew 46%, and reserved capacity is the constraint on how much of it we can serve.
⚙️

Operation Lead — Key Takeaways

  • Commitments have dates as well as totals. Show when payments fall due and which demand assumptions support them.
  • Exiting has more than one cost. Consider fees, lost access, delays and rebuilding effort when assessing how reversible the proposal is.
  • Compare scenarios before committing. Explain how the proposed arrangement performs under higher, lower or later demand.

Tech Lead

R&D is $2.08 billion and it is the hardest money in the company to stop. How do you budget it?

  • A Stage it against committed design wins. R&D is the stickiest money we have — stopping a programme mid-flight loses the socket and the team — so each stage should be tied to a customer commitment that already exists.
  • B Hold the R&D dollar amount flat and let it fall as a share of revenue. It has already gone from 33.9% to 25.3% and the business grew anyway.
  • C Spend up to keep the ratio where the industry expects it. If revenue is $8.2 billion, R&D should scale with it, and cutting the ratio signals retreat.
  • D Concentrate on integrating what we just bought. Celestial and XConn cost about $1.6 billion plus contingent payments through fiscal 2029, and unintegrated acquisitions are the most expensive shelf-ware there is.
💻

Tech Lead — Key Takeaways

  • Stopping and restarting are separate questions. Identify what it would take to pause the program and what capability or time might be lost.
  • Budget around evidence. Explain the milestones and customer assumptions supporting the proposed spending, including what would trigger review.
  • Distinguish dollars from ratios. State what a change in spending as a share of revenue reveals and what it leaves unknown about the program.
🔒

Security Lead

Which failure would make all that committed spending worthless — and where do you set the bar?

  • A Fund security out of whatever is left after the growth programmes are budgeted. We have had no material incident and the year was strong.
  • B Fund export-control compliance and design-IP protection as committed lines, ahead of the discretionary growth programmes — they are what keeps the customers we already have legal to sell to and the designs we already own ours.
  • C Fund geographic resilience — qualifying alternative foundry and assembly locations outside a single region of Taiwan, where most of our supply currently sits.
  • D Fund the cyber programme's maturity against the NIST framework we already follow, and keep the board reporting cadence.
🔒

Security Lead — Key Takeaways

  • Risk spending supports other commitments. Identify which operating assumptions the proposed controls protect and what happens if those assumptions fail.
  • Classify by actual reversibility. Separate contractual obligations from management priorities, and state the time and cost of changing each line.
  • Make resilience a funded decision. Describe the resources, lead time and evidence needed for the chosen response, including what remains exposed.

Account Manager

You do not answer a functional question. After the five leads present, consider money, commitments, exposure, concentration, and engineering.

  • 1 Rank the problems 1–5 by what must be resolved to survive a year when expected AI demand does not arrive. Use each once. Justify #1 in one sentence citing a figure from your packet.
  • 2 Allocate 100 points across the five problems in blocks of 10, totaling exactly 100.
  • 3 Total the committed bucket. Sort major spending lines into committed, sticky and flexible, stating stopping time and cost. Add the committed payments for fiscal 2027 and explain how much of next year is already decided.
🧭

Account Manager — Key Takeaways

  • Total the same period consistently. Identify the major lines, classify them by stopping time and cost, and explain any uncertainty in the fiscal 2027 total.
  • Prioritize for the downside scenario. Ranking sets order; weighting expresses relative emphasis. Defend the choices against a year when expected AI demand does not arrive.
  • Assess the remaining options. Explain what the combined plan leaves adjustable and what events would trigger a revision.

Key takeaway

Return to the airline that revised its demand forecast while its aircraft orders remained. Use the Reversibility Budget to compare what the company expects with what it has promised and how quickly either can change.
