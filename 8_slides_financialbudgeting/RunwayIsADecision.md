# Runway Is a Decision, Not a Number: Budgeting by Reversibility

Everyone has made a budget. Almost nobody has kept one. So we all half-believe a budget is fiction — and the useful correction is that **most of it is fiction and some of it absolutely is not**, and the whole skill is knowing which lines are which. Writing a budget is two different activities called one thing. Some of what you write is an **estimate**: we think we will spend about this much on marketing. Some of it is a **contract**: we have signed a piece of paper, and it will be paid whether or not the year goes the way we said. Estimates flex. Contracts do not. A budget that cannot tell you which is which is not a plan; it is a wish with columns.

That distinction is why budgeting matters more for a new venture than for a large company, and not for the reason students expect. It is not thrift. A startup's defining scarcity is **optionality** — the ability to be wrong and still act — and every commitment converts flexible money into committed money, permanently, for its term. **A budget is a record of how much of the future you have already promised away.** The number at the bottom matters far less than the shape above it, and the shape is a single question: *how much of this can I still change?*

Sort every line into three buckets by asking only how fast you could stop it. **Committed**: signed and contractual — multi-year supply agreements, capacity reservations, leases, debt service, deposits paid. You can sometimes exit, at a price: cancellation fees, forfeited deposits, or a place in a queue you waited years to join. Time to stop: months to never. **Sticky**: not contractual but not fast — people, half-finished engineering programs, factory ramps under way. You can stop these; you just destroy something on the way out, and rebuilding takes as long as building did. **Flexible**: campaigns, travel, contractors, optional projects. Off on Monday, back on in March.

Now the uncomfortable observation. **When the year goes badly, all the pressure falls on the flexible bucket, because it is the only one that moves.** So what gets cut is not what *should* be cut; it is what *can* be. Flexible is usually the smallest bucket and frequently the most valuable per dollar.

The canonical startup financial failure is a single shape wearing different logos: **long, fixed obligations funded by short, cancellable revenue.** Sign fifteen-year leases and sell month-to-month memberships and you have a business that is solvent only while demand holds. Order aircraft in 2019 against a 2019 forecast and take delivery into 2020, when nobody flew; the forecast was revised in a morning and the commitment took years to unwind. Airlines spent the crisis negotiating delivery delays, because the one thing they could not do was un-sign. **You make commitments based on a forecast and you pay them based on reality** — and worse, **demand can cancel on you faster than supply can cancel on you.** Customers buy on purchase orders they can pull with short notice; suppliers sell on contracts you signed for years. Revenue falls immediately and costs fall slowly, and the gap between those two speeds is the exact size of the crisis.

The same shape is being built right now at extraordinary scale. *The New York Times* reported asset managers pouring hundreds of billions into AI data centres, with concerns about **oversupply** and a bubble (Farrell, 2025, The New York Times, via [Techmeme](https://www.techmeme.com/250602/p9)), and described the terms Meta negotiated for its Louisiana data centre, including tax breaks agreed with little public input (2026, The New York Times, via [Techmeme](https://www.techmeme.com/260727/p8)). These are committed-bucket decisions — land, power, multi-year capacity — placed against forecasts nobody can verify, and they reach past the balance sheet: Maine's governor vetoed what would have been the first state pause on data centre construction, citing a project in a distressed mill town (Russell, 2026, The New York Times, via [Techmeme](https://www.techmeme.com/260424/p31)). Once a town's tax base is a line in your plan, reversibility stops being only a financial question.

And the forecast underneath all of it is moving fast. Anthropic's index found that **"over a third of survey respondents expect AI to be able to do most or nearly all of their work tasks in the next 12 months"** (Massenkoff et al., 2026, [Anthropic Research](https://www.anthropic.com/research/economic-index-june-2026-report)). Expectations that steep are exactly the conditions under which committed spending gets signed — and the same team, reviewing **"56 randomized US studies"** of retraining, concluded that **"if AI displaces workers at scale, existing retraining programs would likely fall short"** (Roodman & Massenkoff, 2026, [Anthropic Research](https://www.anthropic.com/research/reviewing-the-evidence-on-worker-retraining-programs)). The adjustment mechanism society is counting on is itself a forecast, and not a strong one. Who bears the cost of that adjustment is what Nick Hanauer and Jason Furman were arguing in August, Hanauer charging that the system **"increasingly rewards ownership over work"** (Hanauer, 2026, [Open to Debate](https://opentodebate.substack.com/p/attend-our-live-debate-is-capitalism)).

So budget by reversibility rather than by percentage of revenue. Total the committed bucket for next year: that is your floor, the spending that happens whatever revenue does. Compare it to your **worst plausible** revenue, not your expected revenue. If the floor is higher, you have not written a budget — you have placed a bet, which may be the right call, but should be a decision rather than a discovery. Then check how much flexible spending remains: that is your entire capacity to respond to being wrong.

Which leaves the question financial analysis still cannot answer:

**How do you price the option you give up when you sign a commitment — and how would you know, before revenue told you, that you had spent your last one?**

Discounted cash flow values the expected path beautifully and says nothing about the worth of being able to change your mind. Scenario analysis names the downside without pricing the flexibility that would let you survive it. So the discipline has to be behavioural, not computational. Ask it before every signature: **if the demand we are assuming does not arrive, how long does it take to stop paying for it — and what does stopping cost?**
