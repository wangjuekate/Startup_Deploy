Case: Marvell Technology, Inc., Form 10-K for the fiscal year ended January 31, 2026

You own one decision: the shape of the commitment ladder. Everyone on your team has a different page. Yours is what has been promised to suppliers. Read it, pick one option, and be ready to defend it with a number.

What the filing says

The ladder

“Future unconditional purchase commitments as of January 31, 2026, are as follows (in millions):”

Fiscal Year Purchase Commitments to Foundries and Test and Assembly Partners Technology Services and License Fees
2027 $1,871.8 $154.6
2028 476.6 169.4
2029 68.6 122.3
2030 66.3 111.7
2031 64.4 43.4
Thereafter 118.1 33.9
Total $2,665.8 $635.3

“In addition, as of January 31, 2026, the Company had approximately $152.0 million of commitments for capital expenditures, the majority of which are expected to be paid within the next twelve months.”

Look at the shape, not just the total. $1,871.8 million of $2,665.8 million — seventy percent of everything ever committed — falls in the single year that has just begun.

What it costs to change your mind

“Under the Company’s manufacturing relationships with its foundry partners, cancellation of outstanding purchase orders is allowed but requires payment of all costs and expenses incurred through the date of cancellation, and in some cases, may result in incremental fees, loss of amounts paid in advance, or loss of priority to reserved capacity for a period of time.”

That last phrase is the expensive one. In a capacity-constrained market, losing your place in the queue can cost more than the cancellation fee.

How long these run

“The Company entered into manufacturing supply capacity reservation agreements with foundries and test and assembly suppliers in prior fiscal years. Under these arrangements, the Company agreed to pay capacity fees or refundable deposits to the suppliers in exchange for reserved manufacturing production capacity over the term of the agreements, which ranges from 4 to 10 years. In addition, the Company committed to certain purchase levels that were in line with the capacity reserved. The Company currently estimates that it has agreed to purchase level commitments of at least $458.2 million of wafers, substrates, and other manufacturing products for fiscal 2027 through fiscal 2033.”

Where it is all made

“We are a fabless supplier of high-performance semiconductor products.”

“Most of our current qualified integrated circuit foundries are located in the same region within Taiwan. In addition, our primary assembly, testing and packaging subcontractors are located in the Pacific Rim region… disruptions to business in Taiwan, whether political, military, natural disasters or other events will adversely impact our business.”

— Item 1A, Risk Factors

What the demand behind it looks like

Net revenue $8,194.6 million (+42.1%); data center +46% on AI demand; communications and other +31%.

And:

“A significant portion of our sales are made on a purchase order basis, which allows our customers to cancel, change or delay product purchase commitments with relatively short notice to us.”

Distributor A was 37% of net revenue (from 34% and 24%). The filing also expects U.S. export restrictions on certain Chinese customers to keep affecting revenue, with some customers “amass[ing] large inventories of our products well in advance of need.”

And the inventory rule that hangs off all of this

“The estimate of future demand is compared to our inventory levels, including open purchase commitments, to determine the amount, if any, of obsolete or excess inventory… A significant decrease in demand could result in an increase in the amount of excess inventory on hand.”

Your question

Seventy percent of every commitment you have falls in one year. What do you do with the ladder?

Your four choices — pick one

  • (A) Diversify where it is made. Most qualified foundries sit in one region of Taiwan and assembly and test in the Pacific Rim — the concentration risk is geographic before it is financial.
  • (B) Re-shape the ladder so commitments are staggered across years instead of stacked. $1,871.8 million of the $2,665.8 million total falling in one year is a single bet on a single demand forecast.
  • (C) Negotiate the exit terms rather than the volumes. If cancellation costs and loss of queue priority are the real penalty, that is what should be renegotiated.
  • (D) Commit more capacity now while it is available. AI demand is here, data center revenue grew 46%, and reserved capacity is the constraint on how much of it we can serve.

Before you answer

A competent lead does four things, in this order:

  1. Name the bucket — is this COMMITTED (months to never), STICKY (a quarter or two, with damage), or FLEXIBLE (stoppable immediately)?
  2. Cite at least one figure from this page. Not a feeling — a number.
  3. Name what your choice gives up. Every option costs you something.
  4. State one thing that would change your answer. If nothing would, you are not reasoning.

Run one scenario out loud before you pick: the demand forecast behind fiscal 2027 is twenty percent too high. What happens to the $1,871.8 million? What happens to inventory? And how much of it can you still stop?
