The Best Year Marvell Ever Had

Fiscal 2026 was, on paper, a triumph.

Net revenue $8,194.6 million — up 42.1% from $5,767.3 M. Gross margin jumped from 41.3% to 51.0%, gross profit to $4,180.7 million. Operating income swung from a (12.5)% loss to 16.1% of revenue; net income reached 32.6% of revenue; operating cash flow hit $1.8 billion. The engine was clear: revenue grew “due to a 46% increase in sales from the data center end market which benefited from strong AI-related demand,” alongside a 31% rise in communications and other.

A 32.6% net margin on 42% growth is the kind of year that gets framed.

Now find the catch: the line called interest income and other, net, at $1,926.3 million against $15.0 million the year before. That is not interest. “On August 14, 2025, we completed the sale of our automotive ethernet business to Infineon Technologies AG for $2.5 billion in cash. During the third quarter of fiscal 2026, we recorded a pre-tax gain on sale of $1.8 billion, which is included in interest income and other, net.”

Almost the entire “other income” line is one asset sale. Strip it out and the 32.6% net margin belongs to a different company. Marvell sold a business and booked the proceeds where they flatter the income statement — legitimately, disclosed plainly, and still capable of fooling anyone who reads the summary rather than the note. The $1.8 billion happens once. The $2,075.2 million of R&D — 25.3% of revenue, down from 33.9% only because revenue outgrew spending — happens every year.

And the cash did not sit still. On February 2, 2026 Marvell acquired Celestial AI, “a provider of a Photonic Fabric™ technology platform purpose-built for next-generation scale-up interconnect,” for “approximately $1.3 billion in cash” plus ~24.5 million shares — and, critically, “Contingent on the achievement of specified revenue milestones, we may be required to pay additional cash and issue additional shares… through fiscal 2029.” On February 10, 2026 it bought XConn Technologies, “a provider of advanced PCIe and CXL switching silicon,” for “approximately $280.0 million in cash” plus ~2.1 million shares.

The one-time gain is spent, and a multi-year contingent obligation has replaced it.

Which brings us to the least reversible page in the filing. Marvell is “a fabless supplier of high-performance semiconductor products” — it designs chips and buys manufacturing, which at this scale means committing years ahead. As of January 31, 2026, future unconditional purchase commitments to foundries and test and assembly partners total $2,665.8 million, and $1,871.8 million of that falls in fiscal 2027 alone — against $476.6 M in fiscal 2028 and $68.6 M in fiscal 2029. Technology services and license fees add $635.3 million.

They do not unwind cleanly. “Under the Company’s manufacturing relationships with its foundry partners, cancellation of outstanding purchase orders is allowed but requires payment of all costs and expenses incurred through the date of cancellation, and in some cases, may result in incremental fees, loss of amounts paid in advance, or loss of priority to reserved capacity.” Cancelling costs money and your place in the queue. Beyond that sit manufacturing supply capacity reservation agreements — fees and deposits “in exchange for reserved manufacturing production capacity over the term of the agreements, which ranges from 4 to 10 years… purchase level commitments of at least $458.2 million… for fiscal 2027 through fiscal 2033.”

Now the sentence that turns all of this into a real problem. On the demand side: “A significant portion of our sales are made on a purchase order basis, which allows our customers to cancel, change or delay product purchase commitments with relatively short notice to us.”

Read those two together. Customers can cancel on short notice; the foundries cannot be cancelled without paying. Obligations are fixed for years; revenue is cancellable in weeks. That asymmetry is the whole budgeting problem, and it is worse than it looks because of who the customers are: Distributor A was 37% of total net revenue in fiscal 2026, up from 34% and 24%; Direct Customer A 14%, up from 13% and under 10%. “The Company’s accounts receivable was concentrated with four customers at January 31, 2026, who represented a total of 73% of gross accounts receivable.” And “sales shipped to customers with operations in Asia represented approximately 77% and 75% of our net revenue” in fiscal 2026 and 2025.

Where it gets made concentrates too: “Most of our current qualified integrated circuit foundries are located in the same region within Taiwan… disruptions to business in Taiwan, whether political, military, natural disasters or other events will adversely impact our business.” One region for supply, one region for most demand.

The policy weather is not helping. “We expect that the U.S. government’s export restrictions on certain Chinese customers to continue to impact our revenue… has caused, and may in the future cause, some of our customers in China to amass large inventories of our products well in advance of need… Customers in China may also choose to develop indigenous solutions.” The company also monitors “potential long-term impact on supply and demand from tariffs.” Stockpiling inflates today’s orders and hollows out tomorrow’s — while the foundry ladder is already signed.

The balance sheet gives room, not immunity. Cash ~$2.6 billion. Total borrowings $4.5 billion, $499.8 million due within twelve months; a 1.5billionrevolversitsundrawn**;interestexpenseran**1.5 billion revolver sits undrawn**; interest expense ran **(202.6) million. Meanwhile the company pays a regular quarterly dividend and runs share repurchases — both “subject to… our results of operations, cash balances and future cash requirements.”

Fiscal 2027 begins with $1,871.8 million already owed to the foundries, roughly $1.6 billion just spent on two acquisitions, contingent payments running to fiscal 2029 — and a customer base that can change its mind with relatively short notice.
