# Secrets, Patents, and People: Is Intellectual Property a Legal System or an Operational One?

Intellectual property protection is not one decision. It is **choosing an instrument and paying that instrument's price**, and there are only four instruments worth knowing. A **patent** grants a right over an invention for a limited term in exchange for publishing it. **Copyright** arises automatically and covers expression, never the underlying idea. A **trademark** protects the name and the mark, potentially forever, as long as you use and defend it. A **trade secret** protects anything valuable that is secret, with no filing and no expiry — but only if you take reasonable measures to keep it secret.

The oldest illustration is still the cleanest. Coca-Cola has never patented its formula; the recipe sits in a vault, protected for over a century by the simple method of not telling anyone. A pharmaceutical company does the opposite, patenting a molecule immediately — which means **publishing** it, handing the world a document specifying exactly what it is and how to make it. Why would anyone do that? Because of one question: **can you tell if somebody is using it?** If a rival ships a pill with your molecule in it, you can buy the pill, test it, and prove it in an afternoon. If a rival ships a cola that tastes similar, you have nothing. Patents work when infringement is visible. **A patent is a right you can enforce against a stranger. A trade secret is a right you can only enforce against someone you trusted.**

For an unreleased product, disclosure costs everything, and that single fact decides the instrument. **The secret and the surprise are the same asset.** Describe an unlaunched device precisely enough to claim it and you have told competitors what to build and spent the launch before it happened — and a patent takes years to grant while the advantage may last eighteen months. So the answer for pre-release work is usually secrecy plus speed, backed by contracts, because **you can decide to publish a secret and you can never decide to un-publish one.** Secrecy is the only option that keeps every other option alive.

In July, Apple filed a complaint in federal court against OpenAI, a hardware venture and two former employees, alleging that information about unreleased products left the company through people it had hired, trained and badged, and through a partner it had trusted (2026, reported via [Techmeme](https://www.techmeme.com/260710/p34)). **Everything in that document is an allegation; the defendants have not answered it here and no court has ruled.** But the *shape* of the allegation is what matters pedagogically, because it is the shape almost every trade-secret case takes. Nobody infringes a trade secret from a distance. It leaves through a person you hired, a partner you briefed, or a supplier you qualified.

That shape answers the question in the title. **Trade-secret protection is not primarily a legal system. It is an operational one** — access control, offboarding, device return, chain of custody, logging, and the discipline of noticing. The lawsuit is what happens *after* the operational system failed, and by then the asset is gone in the only sense that counts: it is in someone else's head, and no injunction removes it. So the question to ask is not *how do we protect this?* but: **"If someone used this tomorrow, would we ever find out — and if we did, what could we prove?"** If the honest answer is *we would never know*, no registered right saves you, and every dollar belongs in detection and access control.

The hardest version of this problem is not in a file at all. The most valuable know-how in most companies lives in the heads of the twenty people who learned, over a decade, which approaches **do not** work. Lawyers call it negative know-how, and it walks out of your building every evening and comes back voluntarily every morning — until one day it does not. You can see the same accumulation in usage data: Anthropic's index found that **"long-tenure users are about 5 percentage points more likely to have a successful conversation"** than newer ones (Massenkoff et al., 2026, [Anthropic Research](https://www.anthropic.com/research/economic-index-march-2026-report)). Experience is a real asset that no filing covers. **The only instruments that touch it are a contract and a culture.**

So when should an entrepreneur publish instead? When infringement would be visible, when the advantage outlives the years a grant takes, and when disclosure does not destroy the thing disclosed. The current copyright fights add a fourth case: **publish when the value is in the expression.** When *The New York Times* sued OpenAI and Microsoft in December 2023, it was not protecting a secret — its journalism is published by design, at scale. The complaint contended that millions of its articles had been used to train systems that now compete with it as a source of information (Grynbaum & Mac, 2023, The New York Times, via [Editor & Publisher](https://www.editorandpublisher.com/stories/the-times-sues-openai-and-microsoft-over-ai-use-of-copyrighted-work,247366)). That is a copyright argument, available only because the work was published, registered and traceable. **Publishing was not the failure of the strategy. It was the strategy** — and the open question is whether the right still functions when copying happens at training scale.

Open to Debate staged that disagreement directly, with Jonathan Taplin arguing that generative systems will hollow out creative work and Rebecca Fiebrink arguing they let more people make more things (Taplin and Fiebrink, 2023, [Open to Debate](https://opentodebate.substack.com/p/will-ai-kill-the-future-of-the-creative-arts)). Both sides accept the work is visible. They disagree about whether visibility still comes with a remedy.

Which leaves the question a founder actually has to answer before shipping anything:

**Which of our advantages survives being described — and for the ones that do not, have we built anything that would tell us they had walked out the door?**

The malpractice is not choosing wrong. Secrecy and publication are both correct, for different assets, on different clocks. **The malpractice is choosing without knowing which trade you just made** — and then discovering, in a courtroom, that the instrument you relied on never covered the thing you actually lost.
