Case: Apple Inc. v. Liu, Tan, OpenAI Foundation, OpenAI Group PBC and io Products, LLC, No. 5:26-cv-07078 (N.D. Cal.), complaint filed July 10, 2026

⚠️ Everything on this page is an allegation from a filed complaint. Nothing has been proved and no court has ruled. Say “the complaint alleges” every time you state a fact. That discipline is graded.

You own one decision: what the company instruments, logs and can prove. Everyone on your team has a different page. Yours is detection. Read it, pick one option, and be ready to defend it with something from the document.

What the complaint alleges

Where every allegation in this document came from

“The following examples, drawn from Apple’s ongoing internal investigation of information gleaned from Apple’s company-owned devices and systems, begin to illuminate the scope and nature of the Defendants’ unlawful conduct.”

— ¶ 54

Then, one sentence later, the limit of it:

“While Apple’s ongoing investigation has provided insights obtained from its own systems, Apple has virtually no visibility into what has been happening behind closed doors at OpenAI and io.”

— ¶ 54

There is your whole job in two sentences. Everything Apple can plead, it can plead because it logged it. Everything it cannot see, it can only ask a court for.

The technical controls Apple pleads it already has

“Network storage access at Apple is provisioned through Apple’s Access Manager system… that limit[s] access to only ‘for work purposes’… Apple disables and prohibits access to its network storage upon an employee’s departure.”

— ¶ 47

“…database exchange and collaboration systems which include permission assignment to ensure access is on a need-to-know basis… and maintaining high standards for encryption, password protection, and device authentication.”

— ¶ 48

What is alleged to have happened anyway

An unreturned device. Mr. Liu is alleged to have “failed to return at least one Apple-owned computer” (¶ 55) and, within hours of leaving, to have told a still-employed colleague “I still have another computer” he planned to use to access Apple’s confidential information (¶ 56).

A borrowed, still-authenticated machine. He allegedly used that colleague’s “Apple-issued work computer that was authenticated to Apple’s network—accessing it while she was still employed at Apple and he was not.” (¶ 56)

Access that survived departure.

“Mr. Liu discovered that, surprisingly, he still could access the Apple’s network repository after leaving Apple, the result of a then-unknown authentication vulnerability. Rather than bringing this to Apple’s attention… Mr. Liu celebrated his find… ‘LOL, I found out I can access the [network storage], so funny.’ Ms. Peng’s response was immediate: ‘I’m ready.’”

— ¶ 57

And then the download.

“Mr. Liu then proceeded to select, access, and download dozens of confidential files from Apple’s network repository… including a compilation of technical files with over a thousand pages… many expressly labeled as confidential.”

— ¶ 58

One of them is described as a presentation on the manufacture and testing of MLBs (main logic boards) that “identifies specific equipment to perform these analyses and issues each piece of equipment is capable of diagnosing” (¶ 59).

How it ended — and how it was found

“Apple discovered Mr. Liu’s misconduct through its own investigation. Once it learned of this security breach, Apple immediately took steps to ensure Mr. Liu’s access was terminated.”

— ¶ 60

“At no point did Mr. Liu notify Apple or take steps to stop it.”

— ¶ 61

And one more, because it tells you what people believe about your logging: he allegedly coached a colleague on how to access and copy files from Apple workstations “to avoid trouble with the security team” (¶ 63). Somebody was already reasoning about what you would detect.

Your question

A secret whose departure you cannot prove is a secret you cannot enforce. Where does engineering effort go?

Your four choices — pick one

  • (A) Instrument the exfiltration paths — repository access logging, anomaly detection around departures, device custody — because every provable allegation in this complaint came from our own systems.
  • (B) Close the specific hole and the class of holes like it: the authentication bug, unreturned devices, and access that survives a departure.
  • (C) Lock the environment down so tightly that files cannot be moved or copied at all, and accept the friction as the cost of secrecy.
  • (D) Harden device management — full-disk encryption, remote wipe, blocking personal email and removable media on work machines.

Before you answer

A competent lead does four things, in this order:

  1. Name the instrument you are reaching for — patent, copyright, trademark, trade secret, contract, or an operational control — and which box of the matrix it sits in.
  2. Answer the detection question: would we ever find out, and what could we prove?
  3. Cite an allegation from this page. Not a feeling — something from the document.
  4. State one thing that would change your answer. If nothing would, you are not reasoning.

One test before you commit. A patent is published, so infringement is visible on the shelf; a trade secret is invisible, so misuse is only visible in your logs. Ask yourself what the complaint would say if Apple had kept no records at all — and then ask which of the four options above changes what tomorrow’s complaint is able to say.
