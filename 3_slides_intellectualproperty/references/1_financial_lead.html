Financial Lead

Detailed reference, not required in full during the timed activity. Start with the short student packet; use this page to investigate or cite supporting evidence.

Case: Apple Inc. v. Liu, Tan, OpenAI Foundation, OpenAI Group PBC and io Products, LLC, No. 5:26-cv-07078 (N.D. Cal.), complaint filed July 10, 2026

⚠️ Everything on this page is an allegation from a filed complaint. Nothing has been proved and no court has ruled. Say “the complaint alleges” every time you state a fact. That discipline is graded.

You own one decision: where the next protection dollar goes. Everyone on your team has a different page. Yours is the money. Read it, pick one option, and be ready to defend it with something from the document.

What the complaint alleges

What is at stake, in Apple’s own pleading

“Apple has invested hundreds of billions of dollars and decades of effort in developing this information and keeping it confidential. A competitor with access to it could bypass years of independent research and development, skip the capital expenditure required to build genuine expertise, and bring products to market faster and at lower cost—harming the value of Apple’s investments.”

— Complaint ¶ 41

“Apple receives substantial benefit from its investment and innovation in its interconnected ecosystem of proprietary information spanning hardware engineering, manufacturing design, supply chain, business operations, and institutional expertise.”

— ¶ 39

What Apple is already spending on

The complaint describes protective measures across four layers — every one of them a budget line somebody already approved:

  • Contractual — an Intellectual Property Agreement signed by every employee; NDAs with suppliers, vendors and contractors before access, “and even then only providing access to confidential information on a need-to-know basis.”
  • Training — an annual “Business Conduct” course.
  • Technical — a secure network repository provisioned through Access Manager; encryption, password protection, device authentication; need-to-know permissioning; access disabled on departure.
  • Physical — “locked buildings, security cameras, guards”; badge access limited to certain buildings; escorts for anyone without a badge.
  • Supply chain — supplier security teams, cameras, ID screening; project code names; “proprietary chain-of-custody protocols for sensitive components to ensure secure disposal of scrap materials”; a proprietary shipping process for prototypes.

The sentence that should decide your page

“Apple has no adequate remedy at law. Unless enjoined, OpenAI will continue to use and benefit from Apple’s misappropriated information.”

— ¶ 124

That is not rhetoric; it is a legal term. It means money cannot fix this. Apple asks instead for injunctions to preserve evidence, to stop further use, and to return all copies — plus exemplary damages under 18 U.S.C. § 1836(b)(3)(C) for conduct alleged to be “willful and malicious.”

The scale of the problem

The complaint alleges over four hundred former Apple employees now work at OpenAI, and that OpenAI’s valuation went “from approximately $29 billion in 2023 to $852 billion by April 2026” having “raised more than $180 billion from investors” — a counterparty with more money than almost any plaintiff.

It also alleges the pattern includes “other former Apple employees who had gone to work for OpenAI [who] emailed themselves Apple’s confidential information to personal accounts on their way out the door.”

And what Apple already tried before spending on lawyers

“In February as its investigation was in its early stages, Apple wrote to OpenAI to raise its concerns… Apple asked OpenAI to discuss what precautions OpenAI was taking to avoid this problem, to investigate it, and to remediate any issues. OpenAI never responded.”

— ¶ 14

Your question

Apple pleads it has “no adequate remedy at law.” Where does the next protection dollar go?

Your four choices — pick one

  • (A) Treat it as a cost of doing business. Over four hundred former employees now work there; we cannot police where people go or what they remember.
  • (B) Into patenting more of what can be patented. A patent is at least enforceable without having to prove somebody took something.
  • (C) Into the enforcement action. A credible, expensive, public case is what makes every contractual protection we have believable to the next employee and the next partner.
  • (D) Into the operational system that keeps secrecy intact — need-to-know access, offboarding, supplier chain of custody. For information whose value dies on disclosure, the lawsuit begins after we have already lost the asset.

Before you answer

A competent lead does four things, in this order:

  1. Name the instrument you are buying — patent, copyright, trademark, trade secret, contract, or an operational control — and which box of the matrix it sits in.
  2. Answer the detection question: would we ever find out, and what could we prove?
  3. Cite an allegation from this page. Not a feeling — something from the document.
  4. State one thing that would change your answer. If nothing would, you are not reasoning.

One question before you commit: what does a dollar of litigation buy that a dollar of access control does not — and what does it fail to buy? Read ¶ 124 again before you answer.
