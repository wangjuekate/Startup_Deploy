Operation Lead

Detailed reference, not required in full during the timed activity. Start with the short student packet; use this page to investigate or cite supporting evidence.

Case: Apple Inc. v. Liu, Tan, OpenAI Foundation, OpenAI Group PBC and io Products, LLC, No. 5:26-cv-07078 (N.D. Cal.), complaint filed July 10, 2026

⚠️ Everything on this page is an allegation from a filed complaint. Nothing has been proved and no court has ruled. Say “the complaint alleges” every time you state a fact. That discipline is graded.

You own one decision: how to protect secrets that live outside your own walls. Everyone on your team has a different page. Yours is the supply chain. Read it, pick one option, and be ready to defend it with something from the document.

What the complaint alleges

Your secrets do not all sit inside your buildings

Apple pleads that its trade secrets include things it has deliberately handed to other companies:

“…proprietary manufacturing processes, custom machinery… proprietary metal alloys, metal-finishing techniques, and material specifications; Design for Manufacturability (‘DFM’) expertise… and proprietary equipment housed at supplier facilities.”

— ¶ 40(b)

“…the identities of the specialized sub-suppliers and vendors who collaborate with Apple… fully integrated, plug-and-play solutions engineered with specialized third-party vendors unique to Apple.”

— ¶ 40(c)

Read that again. Who your suppliers are is itself pleaded as a trade secret. So is the finish on the case.

What Apple already requires of partners

“Suppliers working with Apple must sign restrictive confidentiality agreements… Suppliers are required to implement physical security measures, such as using qualified security teams, security cameras, sensors, personnel identification credential screening, and vehicle markings. Apple uses code names for projects to prevent leaks and requires suppliers to track all confidential materials, including by using proprietary chain-of-custody protocols for sensitive components to ensure secure disposal of scrap materials. Apple also maintains a proprietary shipping process to transport confidential prototypes between facilities.”

— ¶ 51

That is a serious control set. Confidentiality, physical security, identity screening, custody, disposal, transport. Now read what allegedly happened anyway.

The allegation that should decide your page

“As one example, OpenAI, including through io, has accessed, exploited, and used Apple’s secret, proprietary industrial design techniques… related to metal-finishing, at least by having a trusted Apple partner perform Apple’s proprietary, trade secret processes for OpenAI’s benefit. …The partner is bound by agreements with Apple that include use restrictions and confidentiality obligations prohibiting it from doing for other companies the confidential work it does for Apple and from using certain facilities for anyone but Apple.”

— ¶ 80

“OpenAI and its cohorts misled the partner to believe they had Apple’s permission to have the partner carry out the confidential metal-finishing technique for OpenAI’s benefit.”

— ¶ 81

Stop on that sentence. The partner had signed. The partner had the restrictions. The partner, as pleaded, was not trying to break them — he thought he was following them, for the right customer. Every control in ¶ 51 answers the question “is this person allowed to see it?” None of them answers “is this instruction really from Apple?”

And it is alleged not to be one partner

“It has been using misappropriated knowledge of Apple’s confidential relationships, manufacturing and design processes, and proprietary terminology to extract from trusted third parties additional trade secret information and processes.”

— ¶ 79

“OpenAI also has approached at least another trusted Apple supplier… using Apple’s confidential information and internal terminology about manufacturing design and specific component technologies… to ask targeted questions (that only Apple-insiders would know to ask) about specific confidential Apple components.”

— ¶ 83

The stolen information is alleged to be the key that opens the next supplier. Knowing the code name is what makes the question sound authorised.

The commercial context, as pleaded

OpenAI is alleged to have announced a partnership with Foxconn — “Apple’s iPhone assembler” — and engaged Luxshare and Goertek, “both established Apple suppliers.” The people you rely on are the people your competitor is hiring.

Your question

Your secrets live at companies you do not control. Where do you act?

Your four choices — pick one

  • (A) Extend chain of custody and audit to every partner holding our equipment or processes, not only to the components and the scrap.
  • (B) Fix authorisation at the partner boundary — verified requests, named authorised contacts and purpose limits — because the pleaded failure was a partner who believed the instruction came from us.
  • (C) Narrow the circle — fewer partners see any given process, and no partner sees enough of the system to reconstruct it.
  • (D) Restrict which suppliers may work with AI companies at all, as a condition of doing business with us.

Before you answer

A competent lead does four things, in this order:

  1. Name the instrument you are reaching for — patent, copyright, trademark, trade secret, contract, or an operational control — and which box of the matrix it sits in.
  2. Answer the detection question: would we ever find out, and what could we prove?
  3. Cite an allegation from this page. Not a feeling — something from the document.
  4. State one thing that would change your answer. If nothing would, you are not reasoning.

One test before you commit. The partner in ¶ 80 had a signed agreement, security cameras, ID screening and chain of custody — and the process still allegedly ran. So ask the uncomfortable version of the question: which of your controls would have stopped a partner who believed he had your permission? If the answer is “none of them,” you have found the gap, and it is not confidentiality.
