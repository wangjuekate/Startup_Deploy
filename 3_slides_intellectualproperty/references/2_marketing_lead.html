Case: Apple Inc. v. Liu, Tan, OpenAI Foundation, OpenAI Group PBC and io Products, LLC, No. 5:26-cv-07078 (N.D. Cal.), complaint filed July 10, 2026

⚠️ Everything on this page is an allegation from a filed complaint. Nothing has been proved and no court has ruled. Say “the complaint alleges” every time you state a fact. That discipline is graded.

You own one decision: what the company says, and when. Everyone on your team has a different page. Yours is disclosure. Read it, pick one option, and be ready to defend it with something from the document.

What the complaint alleges

The asset you are being asked to talk about

Among the categories Apple pleads as trade secrets:

“proprietary product designs for current and future unreleased products, maintained under strict confidentiality using internal code names and need-to-know access controls; confidential product roadmaps, milestone schedules, and development timelines”

— ¶ 40(a)

Sit with that for a second. For this company, the product and the surprise are the same asset. A roadmap is only worth keeping because nobody else has it.

The company’s whole method is secrecy

“Apple surprises and delights its customers each year with innovative new hardware products, keeping much of its work secret until the moment its finished products are ready to be revealed.”

— ¶ 2

The complaint describes project code names used “to prevent leaks”, a proprietary shipping process “to transport confidential prototypes between facilities”, and badge access “limited to certain buildings or portions of buildings depending on the employee’s specific team and role.”

And here is the sentence that shows why secrecy is not just habit

“These innovations reflect years of research, development, and investment that cannot be readily ascertained through reverse engineering or independent research alone.”

— ¶ 40(c)

“…including the negative know-how of what approaches have been tested, rejected, or refined— that cannot be replicated through reverse engineering or independent research.”

— ¶ 40(d)

A trade secret only survives while it is secret. Nothing you publish comes back.

What is alleged to be happening with that information

The complaint alleges the defendants are “exploiting Apple’s confidential information to advance OpenAI’s efforts to enter the consumer hardware market”, that OpenAI announced a partnership with Foxconn — “Apple’s iPhone assembler” — and engaged Luxshare and Goertek, “both established Apple suppliers”, and that by November 2025 OpenAI had “completed ‘first prototypes’ of its device.”

It also alleges Mr. Tan used “an Apple internal project codename to ask, ‘What’s the plan[?]’ for an unannounced Apple product” during job interviews with Apple employees.

What Apple has now made public — by filing

A complaint is a public document. By filing it, Apple has published: the categories of information it protects, the measures it takes, the fact that it wrote to OpenAI in February and that “OpenAI never responded”, and the specific conduct it alleges — including that candidates were allegedly directed to bring “Actual parts” to interviews for “show and tell” sessions, and that one candidate said he “didn’t even know we could take those from the office.”

What Apple explicitly did not touch

“Apple and OpenAI have a commercial relationship involving the integration of OpenAI’s ChatGPT into Apple Intelligence… That agreement is not at issue here.”

— ¶ 53 n.13

Somebody on your team will want to make that part of the story. The complaint deliberately does not.

Your question

When the secret and the surprise are the same asset, what does the company say — and when?

Your four choices — pick one

  • (A) Say as little as possible about any of it, including the litigation. Attention on the case is attention on what was taken.
  • (B) Say nothing about the products and everything about the consequences. For unreleased hardware the surprise IS the asset; what we can publicise for free is what happens to people who take it.
  • (C) Make the protection programme visible to candidates, partners and suppliers — people comply with rules they know exist and have seen enforced.
  • (D) Pre-announce roadmaps and milestones to establish public priority, so nobody can later claim they got there first.

Before you answer

A competent lead does four things, in this order:

  1. Name the instrument you are reaching for — patent, copyright, trademark, trade secret, contract, or an operational control — and which box of the matrix it sits in.
  2. Answer the detection question: would we ever find out, and what could we prove?
  3. Cite an allegation from this page. Not a feeling — something from the document.
  4. State one thing that would change your answer. If nothing would, you are not reasoning.

One test before you commit. Publishing is how patents work. Silence is how secrets work. Which one is this company running on — and what happens to the asset the day you announce it to protect it?
