A Secret and a Contract

Apple holds more patents than almost any company on earth. When it went to court in July 2026, it used none of them.

The caption reads “COMPLAINT FOR TRADE SECRET MISAPPROPRIATION AND BREACH OF CONTRACT” — jury trial demanded, jurisdiction under the Defend Trade Secrets Act, 18 U.S.C. §§ 1836 et seq. Six claims: DTSA misappropriation against each defendant, and breach of Apple’s Intellectual Property Agreement against two individuals. Not a patent, a copyright or a trademark. A secret, and a contract. The first sentence is blunt: “This case is about Apple’s former employees stealing Apple’s trade secrets for the benefit of OpenAI.”

The reason for that choice is the whole lesson. A patent is a bargain — the state grants exclusivity and you publish how it works. What Apple says it is protecting cannot survive publication. It pleads five categories of trade secret, each said to “derive independent economic value from its secrecy”: hardware engineering and product design, including “proprietary product designs for current and future unreleased products” and “confidential product roadmaps, milestone schedules”; manufacturing and industrial design, including “proprietary metal alloys, metal-finishing techniques” and Design for Manufacturability expertise; component technologies, including “the identities of the specialized sub-suppliers and vendors who collaborate with Apple”; testing methodology, including the “negative know-how of what approaches have been tested, rejected, or refined—that cannot be replicated through reverse engineering”; and supply-chain operations, where “Apple’s systems-level integration knowledge… is a trade secret in its own right.”

“Apple has invested hundreds of billions of dollars and decades of effort in developing this information and keeping it confidential,” the complaint says. A competitor with access could “bypass years of independent research and development… and bring products to market faster.”

Here is the catch that makes this a management case, not a legal one. A trade secret is only a trade secret if the owner took reasonable measures to keep it secret. Protection is not a filing; it is an operating system, and Apple pleads its own in detail: contractual — every employee signs an Intellectual Property Agreement barring use or disclosure “during or after employment” and barring taking “any documents, materials, or copies thereof” on departure, with suppliers signing NDAs before receiving access, “only… on a need-to-know basis”; training — an annual “Business Conduct” course; departure procedures — devices and badges returned, network access disabled; technical — files in a secure repository provisioned through Apple’s Access Manager, encryption, device authentication, need-to-know permissioning; physical — “locked buildings, security cameras, guards,” badge-limited access, escorts; supply chain — project code names, “proprietary chain-of-custody protocols for sensitive components to ensure secure disposal of scrap materials.” And so, Apple pleads, its secrets are “not available to the public or to Apple’s competitors through any legitimate means.”

That is the system the complaint alleges failed at every layer touching a human being.

Two individuals are named. Chang Liu worked at Apple “for over eight years… Senior System Electrical Engineer for Apple’s iPhone product line,” leaving January 2026. Tang Yew Tan “spent twenty-four years at Apple, most recently as Vice President of Product Design for iPhone and Apple Watch. Today he is OpenAI’s Chief Hardware Officer.” Both signed the IPA.

Mr. Liu allegedly “failed to return an Apple-issued work laptop,” remarking “I still have another computer”; used “his former colleague’s Apple-issued work computer… without Apple’s authorization”; and “exploited a rare, previously unknown authentication bug to access Apple’s shared network folders” — and “did not report it.” He allegedly downloaded “dozens of Apple’s confidential hardware-related files.”

Mr. Tan allegedly “began emailing himself information about Apple’s suppliers,” used “an Apple internal project codename” in interviews to ask “What’s the plan[?]” about an unannounced product, and “directed job candidates still working for Apple to bring ‘Actual parts’ from Apple to their interviews for ‘show and tell’ sessions.” One candidate reportedly said he “didn’t even know we could take those from the office.” OpenAI, the complaint alleges, instructs candidates to bring “CAD/design artifacts” and “prototypes” and to divulge “subsystem and component selection” and “vendor selection and communication with vendors.”

The measure protecting the departure process allegedly leaked too: an internal managers’ document marked “Need to Know”, describing Apple’s own security procedures for employee departures, was allegedly shared with new hires “before they give notice to Apple of their departures, previewing Apple’s security protocols.”

The perimeter did not hold outside the company either. A partner allegedly “carr[ied] out a specific trade secret metal-finishing technique for OpenAI, misleading the partner to believe they had Apple’s permission to do so.” That partner was authorised to do the work — for Apple. Nobody checked who was asking.

Why now. OpenAI “transformed from a nonprofit to a commercial enterprise,” its valuation moving “from approximately $29 billion in 2023 to $852 billion by April 2026.” In 2025 it partnered with Foxconn, “Apple’s iPhone assembler,” and engaged Luxshare and Goertek, “both established Apple suppliers.” By November 2025 it had “first prototypes.” Over four hundred former Apple employees now work there. In May 2025 it acquired io Products, LLC — co-founded in 2024 by Mr. Tan and other former senior Apple executives as “the dedicated hardware vehicle for OpenAI” — for approximately $6.5 billion, pleaded as “an alter ego of OpenAI.” In February, Apple wrote raising concerns. “OpenAI never responded.”

So Apple asks for damages, exemplary damages under 18 U.S.C. § 1836(b)(3)(C) for misappropriation alleged to be “willful and malicious,” and — the part that matters more — injunctive relief: orders to preserve evidence, to stop “further acquiring, possessing, using, or disclosing” the trade secrets, and to return all copies. Because, it says, “Apple has no adequate remedy at law.”

One thing the complaint sets aside: Apple and OpenAI still have a commercial relationship integrating ChatGPT into Apple Intelligence. “That agreement is not at issue.”

The record, as pleaded

  • Claim: “COMPLAINT FOR TRADE SECRET MISAPPROPRIATION AND BREACH OF CONTRACT” — DTSA, 18 U.S.C. §§ 1836 et seq., plus breach of the Intellectual Property Agreement (IPA) against the two individuals. Six claims for relief; jury trial demanded.
  • Individuals: Chang Liu — over eight years at Apple, Senior System Electrical Engineer, iPhone product line, left January 2026. Tang Yew Tan — 24 years at Apple, most recently VP of Product Design for iPhone and Apple Watch, now OpenAI’s Chief Hardware Officer. Both signed the IPA.
  • Entities: OpenAI Foundation (Delaware nonprofit), OpenAI Group PBC (Delaware public benefit corporation), and io Products, LLC — acquired May 2025 for ~$6.5 billion, pleaded as an “alter ego” of OpenAI.
  • Reasonable measures pleaded: contractual (IPA, supplier NDAs before access, need-to-know); training (annual Business Conduct course); departure (device/badge return, network access disabled); technical (Access Manager repository, need-to-know permissions, encryption, device authentication); physical (locked buildings, cameras, guards, badges, escorts); supply chain (code names, chain-of-custody protocols, secure scrap disposal).
  • Relief sought: damages; exemplary damages under 18 U.S.C. § 1836(b)(3)(C) for “willful and malicious” misappropriation; injunctions to preserve evidence, to cease “further acquiring, possessing, using, or disclosing”, and to return all copies; “no adequate remedy at law.”
  • Context: OpenAI valuation “$29 billion in 2023 to $852 billion by April 2026”; “more than $180 billion” raised; Foxconn partnership 2025; Luxshare and Goertek engaged; “first prototypes” November 2025; 400+ former Apple employees at OpenAI.
