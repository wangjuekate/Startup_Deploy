Intellectual Property Protection

MENT 3235 · Module 3 · Concept Lecture · Tuesday, September 22

Case: Apple v. OpenAI (N.D. Cal., filed 2026) · Mental model: the Appropriability Matrix

The question is not “how do I protect my idea.” It is “which instrument, and what does that instrument cost me.” Everything about the case is an allegation — set that rule now, before the first slide.

Somebody here has an idea they will not tell anyone

  • Because they are afraid it will get stolen. I want to fix that today — and not by reassuring you. By making the fear precise.
  • “Protect my idea” is not a plan.

Four instruments, working completely differently

Picking the wrong one is worse than picking none — because the wrong one can require you to publish the very thing you were trying to hide.

Expect nervous laughs on the first line. Do not name the four instruments yet; the story does it better than the list does.

The formula and the pill

Coca-Cola has never patented the formula

The recipe sits in a vault. A very small number of people know it. The company has protected it for over a century by the simple method of not telling anyone.

A pharmaceutical company patents immediately

Which means it publishes — a document saying exactly what the molecule is and how to make it. Anyone can read it. In twenty years, anyone can make it.

Why on earth would you do that?

Because of one question: can you tell if somebody is using it? Buy the rival's pill, test it, prove it in an afternoon. Patents work when infringement is visible. A cola that tastes similar proves nothing.

Put the question to the room before you answer it. Someone always says “because it is a better molecule” — the answer is detectability, and that surprises them.

That is the trade

A patent gives you a right you can enforce, in exchange for telling everybody. A trade secret lets you keep quiet, in exchange for having no right against anyone who figures it out honestly.

Reverse-engineer a trade secret legally? Perfectly allowed. Independently invent it? Allowed.

The only thing a trade secret protects against is somebody taking it improperly — which means your protection is only as good as your ability to prove it left, and how.

Slow down on the last sentence. It is the bridge to the complaint they read on Thursday, where the whole fight is about proving how something left.

The four instruments, honestly

Patent

A right over an invention, for a limited term, in exchange for publication. Costs money, takes years, needs infringement to be detectable.

Copyright

Automatic and long. Covers the expression, never the underlying idea. Excellent for text, code, film, images. Useless for a process.

Trademark

The name and the mark, potentially forever — as long as you use it and defend it. Protects who you are, not what you made.

Trade secret

Anything of value that is secret, for as long as it stays secret, with no filing and no term — but only with reasonable measures. Lose secrecy and you lose the asset, permanently.

Go fast here. You need the vocabulary, not the law degree.

Do not let this become a legal lecture. Ninety seconds, then move — the next slide is the one that does the work.

The three questions that decide which one

1 · Detection

Could I tell, from the outside, that somebody is using it? If yes, a registered right is enforceable. If no, it is a public instruction manual with no enforcement.

2 · Life of the advantage

If the field moves in eighteen months, a patent that takes three years to grant and publishes on day one is a bad trade. Speed can be the protection.

3 · Cost of disclosure

Some things lose all value the moment they are described. An unreleased product is the purest case: the secret and the surprise are the same asset.

And the fourth question, which is really the whole lecture

What is actually leaving — the document, or the person?

Beat before the fourth question. Slow down. This is the one that reframes the case from a filing dispute into an operational failure.

What walks out at five o'clock

  • The most valuable know-how in most companies is not in a file.
  • It is in the heads of the twenty people who learned, over a decade, which approaches do not work. Lawyers call that negative know-how.
  • It walks out of your building every evening and comes back voluntarily every morning — until one day it does not.

No instrument reaches it

You cannot patent it. You cannot copyright it.

The only instruments that touch it are a contract and a culture — and both are only as strong as your ability to notice.

Ask what their own team's negative know-how is after four weeks of a project. Most teams realise they already have some and have never written it down.

The mental model — the Appropriability Matrix

Place the asset first. The instrument follows from the box — never the other way around.

Beat 1: the two axes, and nothing else. Make them place one asset of their own before you reveal a single box. Beat 6 is the sentence they should leave with.

The asymmetries that make it a model

Who you can enforce against

A patent is a right you can enforce against a stranger. A trade secret is a right you can only enforce against someone you trusted.

Nobody infringes your trade secret from a distance. It leaves through a person you hired, a partner you briefed, a supplier you qualified. So trade-secret protection is not a legal strategy — it is an operational one: access control, offboarding, chain of custody, logging. The lawsuit is what happens after the operational system already failed.

The one-way door

You can decide to publish a secret. You can never decide to un-publish one.

Secrecy is the only option that keeps every other option alive — which is why, when disclosure destroys the value, secrecy plus speed is usually the right first move, and why losing it once loses it forever.

The left box is the one that changes behaviour on Monday. Ask what their offboarding process is; nobody has one.

The one question the model makes you ask

Not “how do we protect this?” — but if someone used this tomorrow, would we ever find out — and if we found out, what could we prove?

Protecting intellectual property is not one decision. It is choosing an instrument, and paying that instrument's price.

Publish and you can enforce. Stay quiet and you keep the surprise. Both are correct — for different assets, in different companies, on different clocks.

The malpractice is not choosing wrong. It is choosing without knowing which trade you just made.

Have them apply the question to one asset in their own venture. “We would never find out” is the most common honest answer, and it decides the instrument for them.
