# The Take-Rate Waterfall: Which Costs Get Thicker When You Grow

Everyone can describe a business model in one sentence. *We take a cut. We charge a subscription. It's a marketplace, we keep fifteen percent.* That is the first line of the model, and there are eleven more underneath it, every one of which takes money away from you. The sentence is the top of a waterfall, and **operations is the discipline of what happens on the way down.**

The waterfall reads top to bottom. At the top sits volume — gross merchandise value, bookings, units, whatever passes through your hands. Your stated take rate converts some fraction of it into revenue. Then come the subtractions that scale with each transaction: credit losses, payment processing, funding costs, cost of goods. What survives that is **contribution** — the only number that tells you whether growth is worth having. Below contribution sit the fixed slices: technology, marketing, administration. What survives *those* is profit, and in a healthy-looking marketplace it is frequently a rounding error on the number at the top.

The classic illustration is the ninety-nine-cent song. When the iTunes Store launched, about seventy cents of each sale went straight to the record label. Card networks then charged a percentage *plus a fixed fee per transaction* — brutal arithmetic on a ninety-nine-cent sale. Then bandwidth, then the store itself. Estimates at the time put Apple's margin on a song at around a dime. Apple was running the most talked-about storefront on earth and earning almost nothing on the product, because **it was not running the store to make money on songs. It was running it to sell iPods.** Read only the top line and you would call that a music business. It was a device business, and the music was bait.

So which costs get thicker as you grow, and which get thinner? The sorting rule is simply whether the cost is charged per transaction. **Variable slices — credit losses, processing, funding, cost of goods — do not dilute with scale. They multiply with it.** Double your volume and you double the leak. **Fixed slices — engineering, brand marketing, the finance team — grow slower than volume**, and those are the only ones scale genuinely helps. This is why *"it just needs scale"* is sometimes the smartest sentence in the room and sometimes a company's obituary — and why you must know which slice you mean before you say it.

The clean modern demonstration of a thin fixed stack ran in *The New York Times* this April: a telehealth company that recorded $401 million of sales in 2025 with two employees, assembling its site, support, marketing and analytics out of AI tools costing a few hundred dollars a month (Griffith, 2026, The New York Times, via [Techmeme](https://www.techmeme.com/260402/p12)). Notice precisely what collapsed there. **The fixed stack collapsed.** The drug cost, the payment processing, and the advertising spend that buys each customer did not — those are per-transaction, and they grow with every dollar of new volume. A company can therefore look astonishing at the top and ordinary at the bottom, and the gap is entirely a story about which slices it actually removed.

Anthropic's survey of roughly 81,000 people found the most common productivity gain was reach rather than speed: **"the most common productivity enhancement is in scope, which was cited by 48% of users who explicitly mentioned productivity effects"** (Massenkoff & Huang, 2026, [Anthropic Research](https://www.anthropic.com/research/81k-economics)). Scope is a fixed-cost story — one person covering work that used to need a department. It lowers the floor of your fixed stack and does nothing to your variable slices.

Then the three-way trade-off that decides lending-shaped businesses. **Growth, credit quality, and merchant concentration cannot all be optimised at once.** Growth is easiest to buy by approving more marginal customers, which raises credit losses — a variable slice, so the damage arrives in proportion to the growth that caused it. Tightening credit protects contribution and slows volume, which makes your growth rate look worse to your investors. And concentrating volume in a few large merchant partners buys cheap growth at the price of terms: the partner who supplies a third of your volume eventually renegotiates your take rate, and you will agree. It is the operator's version of Nick Hanauer's charge, debating Jason Furman in August, that the system **"increasingly rewards ownership over work"** (Hanauer, 2026, [Open to Debate](https://opentodebate.substack.com/p/attend-our-live-debate-is-capitalism)). Whoever owns the demand sets the split; whoever merely processes it earns a wage. **Every one of those is the same decision wearing different clothes — how much of your contribution margin are you willing to pay for a bigger top line.**

Which gives a usable rule for deciding what to out-operate rather than outgrow. **A fixed cost is a problem you can outgrow. A variable cost is a problem you must out-operate.** If a cost falls per unit as volume rises, growth is a real strategy and patience is rational. If it does not, no amount of volume rescues you and the only lever is the operating decision behind it: who you approved, how you routed the payment, where you borrowed, what you paid for the goods. Two companies can quote the identical take rate and one keeps a third of it while the other keeps nothing — and the difference is never the pitch. **You negotiate your take rate once. You earn your contribution margin every single day.**

Which leaves the question every operator eventually has to answer out loud:

**At what volume does this become profitable — and is it because a fixed cost finally got spread thin, or because we actually fixed a leak?**

If the honest answer is the second, the growth in front of you is worth having. If it is the first, you should be able to name the volume and the date. And if you can name neither, what you are calling a growth plan is a bet that someone else's cost curve will bail you out — which occasionally happens, and never on schedule.
