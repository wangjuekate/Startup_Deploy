The Year Affirm Made Fifty-Two Million Dollars

For most of its life, Affirm has been a company that loses money on purpose.

The proposition is simple enough to explain at a checkout page: buy the thing now, pay for it over time, and if you miss a payment nobody piles on a fee. The company says so plainly — since founding it has charged “$0 in late fees for missed payments. We do not profit from consumers’ mistakes.” That promise is not decoration. It removes the single most reliable revenue stream in consumer credit, which means every dollar Affirm earns has to come from correctly guessing whether a stranger will pay.

In the fiscal year ended June 30, 2025, the guessing finally paid. $36.7 billion of goods moved through the platform — up 38% from $26.6 B, and 81% above the $20.2 B of two years before. Total revenue, net: $3,224.4 million, up 39%. Fifty-two million dollars on thirty-six point seven billion of volume — a real profit, and a very thin one. Understanding why is the whole case.

Start with a single GMV dollar. About 8.8 cents of it becomes revenue — the company is careful to say “GMV does not represent revenue earned by us.” Transaction costs then take roughly 4.75 cents of every GMV dollar, leaving about 4.0 cents. Four costs do the taking, all four scaling with volume: provision for credit losses $616.7 million, processing and servicing $457.8 M, funding costs $425.5 M, and a loss on loan purchase commitment of $242.3 million. Growth does not dilute any of them. Growth multiplies all of them.

Now look at where the money actually comes from, because it is not where most people assume. The largest revenue line is interest income at $1,608.2 million, up 34% — larger than merchant network revenue ($882.7 M) and more than four times gain on sales of loans ($381.6 M, up 94%). Affirm gets paid mostly by the consumer, not the merchant.

That single fact opens the fork the company now lives on. Every loan can go one of two ways. Held, it produces interest income for years, consumes capital, and carries its own funding cost and credit provision. Sold — and $15.8 billion of unpaid principal went out the door this year, up 54% — it produces a gain plus servicing income and consumes almost nothing. Average loans held for investment ran $6.5 billion (+28%) against average funding debt of $5.9 billion (+30%), with an allowance for credit losses at 5.6% of loans held for investment. The company purchased $30.0 billion of loans from its originating bank partners to feed the machine. Hold or sell is not a philosophy. It is a decision made over and over, at scale, and the two answers have completely different economics.

The second pressure is that volume is not evenly spread. Affirm reaches approximately 377 thousand active merchants — and the top five merchants and platform partners were approximately 47% of total GMV, up from 42% two years earlier. Amazon alone was 22%. Half the business sits with five counterparties, and the filing notes the direction of travel: “Some merchants are increasingly offering proprietary pay-over-time options to customers, and in some cases, these are presented parallel to our offerings at checkout.” The partner whose checkout page you live on can build your product.

Against that, the consumer side is quietly compounding. 23.0 million active consumers (+23%), each transacting 5.8 times a year — up from 4.9, and from 3.9 before that. Average order value fell from $292 to $273, which the company frames as a win: “driven by the diversification of our merchant base… and our ongoing initiative to drive repeat usage of our platform beyond one-time high AOV purchases.” The Affirm Card is now ~10% of total transactions, from ~8% and ~2%. And sales and marketing spend fell 25%, from $576.4 M to $434.8 million, while GMV grew 38%.

The product mix carries its own trap. Interest-bearing monthly installments are 72% of GMV; Pay-in-X is 14%; and 0% APR loans are 13% of GMV — $4.7 billion, up 63%. The company notes that “we generally earn larger merchant fees on 0% APR financing products.” Those are also the loans purchased above fair value — the ones generating that $242.3 M loss on loan purchase commitment. The fastest-growing product is the one whose cost shows up somewhere other than where its revenue does.

Underneath all of it is the underwriting. “We believe that our technology, underwriting, and risk management are key competitive advantages. Our proprietary technology’s ability to price and assess risk at a transaction level provides a unique advantage.” The models are “built on extensive data points, including data from approximately 343 million loans.” Technology and data analytics cost $589.7 million (+18%) — more than ten times the year’s profit.

And the licence to operate is borrowed. Affirm relies on “our originating bank partners’ authority under federal law to establish interest rates.” Cross River Bank “generally allows a consumer loan borrower to agree to any annual rate of interest up to 30%,” Celtic Bank up to 36%. Its NIST-framework security program runs penetration tests and red teams and takes “a risk-based approach” to third parties — and states that while nothing has been materially affected so far, “we cannot provide assurance that they will not be materially affected in the future.”

The competition is larger. Visa, Mastercard, PayPal, Block, Klarna — “many of our competitors are substantially larger than we are,” with “the ability to cross-subsidize their offerings through their other business lines” and “lower-cost funding.”

Fifty-two million dollars of profit is the smallest number on the page, and it is the only one that can go back to zero.
