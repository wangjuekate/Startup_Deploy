Case: Affirm Holdings, Inc., Form 10-K for the fiscal year ended June 30, 2025

You own one decision: where the loans live. Everyone on your team has a different page. Yours is the balance sheet. Read it, pick one option, and be ready to defend it with a number.

What the filing says

Two ways to fund the same loan

When a consumer buys something using Affirm, a loan is created. That loan has to sit somewhere, and there are two places it can go.

Keep it. The company holds the loan and collects the interest.

Interest income for fiscal 2025 was $1,608.2 million, up 34% — “generally, interest income is correlated with the changes in the average balance of loans held for investment, which increased by 28% to $6.5 billion.”

Holding is not free. Two costs come attached:

Funding costs $425.5 million (+24%) — “interest expense and the amortization of fees for certain borrowings collateralized by our loans.” Average funding debt from warehouses and securitizations was $5.9 billion, up 30%.

Provision for credit losses $616.7 million (+34%) — “management’s estimate of future losses.” The allowance was 5.6% of loans held for investment, up from 5.5%.

Sell it. The company sells the loan to a third-party buyer or a securitization and keeps servicing it.

Gain on sales of loans was $381.6 million, up 94% — “driven by higher loan sale volume to third-party loan buyers and favorable transaction economics.” The company sold loans with an unpaid principal balance of $15.8 billion in fiscal 2025, up from $10.2 billion (+54%).

Servicing income was $120.6 million (+26%), earned as a percentage of the unpaid principal balance of loans owned by others — which averaged $6.6 billion, up 36%.

How the company describes its own approach

“We proactively manage the allocation of loans on our platform across various funding channels based on several factors including, but not limited to, internal risk limits and policies, capital market conditions and channel economics.”

— Item 7, Liquidity and Capital Resources

Available liquidity: $2.2 billion of cash and equivalents, warehouse and revolving facilities, securitization trusts, forward-flow arrangements, and a variable funding note permitting borrowing “up to an aggregate of $1.4 billion.”

The company purchased $30.0 billion of loans from its originating bank partners during the year, up 40%. Of that, $4.4 billion were 0% APR loans, up 65% — and 0% APR loans are the ones purchased above fair value, which is what creates the $242.3 million loss on loan purchase commitment.

Your question

Every loan has to live somewhere. Where should the loans go?

Your four choices — pick one

  • (A) Grow GMV as fast as we can and fund it wherever there is capacity. GMV grew 38% and revenue grew 39% — the funding sorts itself out behind the volume.
  • (B) Lean toward selling — $15.8 billion sold produced $381.6 million of gain plus $120.6 million of servicing income without tying up our own balance sheet.
  • (C) Allocate each loan to the channel where its own economics win — hold the ones whose spread beats funding cost and expected losses, sell the rest. The channel is a per-loan decision, not a company-wide policy.
  • (D) Lean toward holding — interest income is $1.6 billion, our largest single revenue line, and holding the loans is the only way we keep it.

Before you answer

A competent lead does four things, in this order:

  1. Name the slice of the waterfall your choice touches — and say whether it is variable (grows with volume) or fixed (diluted by volume).
  2. Cite at least one figure from this page. Not a feeling — a number.
  3. Name what your choice gives up. Every option costs you something.
  4. State one thing that would change your answer. If nothing would, you are not reasoning.

Somebody will tell you interest income is the biggest revenue line, so keep the loans. Somebody else will tell you selling is capital-light, so sell them all. Before you agree with either: pick one loan and ask whether its interest covers its funding cost and its share of the $616.7 million provision.
