Operations and the Business Model

MENT 3235 · Module 2 · Concept Lecture · Tuesday, September 15

Case: Affirm FY2025 · Mental model: the Take-Rate Waterfall

Last week they found where the money pools. This week they find where it leaks. Say that in the first thirty seconds — it is the whole relationship between the two lectures.

You have described the first line

  • Everybody here can describe a business model in one sentence. “We take a cut.” “We charge a subscription.” “It's a marketplace, we take fifteen percent.”
  • Congratulations. That is the first line. There are eleven more, and every one of them takes money away from you.

What that sentence actually is

It is the top of a waterfall. The whole discipline of operations is what happens on the way down.

Today: how to read a business model the way an operator reads it — downward, one subtraction at a time, until you reach the number that is actually yours.

Take three business models from the room out loud before you show the box. Write them on the board; you will subtract from them later.

The ninety-nine-cent song

  • When the iTunes Store launched, a song cost ninety-nine cents. Simple, famous, clean.
  • About seventy cents went straight to the record label — wholesale, non-negotiable, they own the music.
  • Then card processing — and here is the cruel part: networks charge a fixed fee per transaction plus a percentage. On a ninety-nine cent sale, the fixed part is brutal.
  • Then bandwidth. Then the store itself. Estimates put Apple's margin at around ten cents. Maybe less.

So why bother?

They were not running the store to make money on songs. They were running it to sell iPods.

The store was a cost of goods sold for the hardware. The music was the bait.

Read only the top line — “Apple sells a billion songs!” — and you conclude they are in the music business. They were in the device business.

Ask them to guess Apple's margin before you say it. Nobody guesses ten cents. That gap is the lecture.

Every subtraction is somebody's job

Payment processing

costs what it costs because of how you route transactions.

Credit losses

cost what they cost because of who you said yes to.

Funding

costs what it costs because of where you borrowed.

Fulfillment

costs what it costs because of where you put the warehouse.

Two companies both say “we take a 3% merchant fee.” One keeps a third of it. One keeps nothing. The difference is not the pitch — it is a thousand operational decisions stacked on top of each other.

The model tells you where the money comes from. Operations tells you how much of it you keep.

This is why the module is called operations and not finance. None of those lines is an accounting entry; each is a process with a person's name on it.

The trap: growth multiplies the whole waterfall

  • Every subtraction is a percentage of volume. Double the volume and you double the leak.
  • Students, founders and honestly a lot of investors look at a company losing money at scale and say: “it just needs scale.”
  • Sometimes that is exactly right — some costs are fixed, and scale spreads them.

And sometimes it is suicide

If what you are losing money on is a per-transaction cost, scale does not fix it. Scale makes it bigger.

You do not grow your way out of a model that leaks per unit. You grow your way deeper into it — faster, with more enthusiasm and more press coverage.

The only honest question: at what volume does this become profitable — and is that because something got spread thinner, or because I fixed a leak?

Change tone here. This is the mistake that shows up in half the team projects, and they will not recognise it as the same mistake unless you name it now.

The mental model — the Take-Rate Waterfall

The bars are to scale. That collapse is the business model — everything else is commentary.

Beat 1: the number in the press release. Beat 2: pause on how little survives the take. Beat 3: name the operator behind each slice. Beat 4 is the line that matters. Beat 5 is Thursday's argument.

How to read it — in order

1 · Write the top number

Volume. GMV, bookings, units — whatever passes through.

2 · Write the take

What fraction becomes revenue? The number people quote, and almost never the interesting one.

3 · List every variable slice

And the operational decision that controls it. If you cannot name the decision, you cannot manage the cost.

4 · Draw the line at contribution

Revenue minus everything that grows with volume. This is the number that says whether growth is worth having.

5 · Only then, the fixed stack

Technology, marketing, administration — and ask what volume makes it work.

The two kinds of slice

Variable slices scale with volume — growth multiplies them. Fixed slices scale slower — these are the ones scale actually helps.

Step 3 is the whole exercise. Make a student name the person who owns each slice; if the room cannot, they have found the real finding.

The asymmetry that makes it a model

Outgrow, or out-operate

A fixed cost is a problem you can outgrow. A variable cost is a problem you must out-operate.

That is why “we'll fix it with scale” is sometimes brilliant and sometimes suicide — and why you must know which slice you are talking about before you say it.

And who gets to choose

You negotiate your take rate once. You earn your contribution margin every single day.

The top is a contract. The middle is a thousand daily choices — approve or decline, route this way or that, hold the asset or sell it. Anyone can copy your contract. Almost nobody can copy four years of operating discipline.

If they take one sentence out of this hour, make it the first one. It is also the sentence that decides the Thursday vote.

The one question the model makes you ask

Not “how much volume will it add?” — but which slice does this volume have to survive, and does that slice get thinner or thicker as we scale?

A billion dollars of volume carrying a five-cent variable cost against an eight-cent take is a good day.

The same billion at a nine-cent variable cost is a press release, followed by a funding round you did not want to raise.

Run their own venture's next growth idea through this question. Refuse any answer that only names the top line.

The one line to carry in

A business model is not a sentence. It is a waterfall.

Somebody hands you volume at the top. Every operational decision you have ever made takes a slice on the way down. What is left at the bottom is the only number that was ever yours.

Operations is the discipline of deciding which slices you can shrink — and knowing which ones get bigger the moment you grow.

Return to the three business models on the board from the opening and subtract out loud. It takes ninety seconds and it lands the whole hour.

Your job

Not to admire the growth rate. To decide.

Before every decision, say which slice it touches — and whether that slice thickens when volume grows.

Hand out the role pages. The Account Manager weights the five leads' questions; that weighting is where the waterfall becomes an argument.
