# Where the Value Actually Sits: Reading a Value Chain for the Step Nobody Can Copy

A value chain is the sequence of steps that stands between a raw thing and a satisfied customer. Somebody sources it. Somebody verifies it. Somebody prices it, moves it, sells it, and handles it when it comes back. Drawn on a whiteboard it looks democratic — a row of boxes, each one necessary, each one doing its share. That picture is wrong in the only way that matters. **Margin does not spread across a chain. It pools.** It collects at one or two steps and runs straight past the rest, and the steps it runs past are not bad steps. They are simply steps where you have no bargaining power, because if you charged more the customer would do it themselves or buy it from the eleven other firms that do it.

The canonical demonstration is IBM in 1980. Building its personal computer quickly, IBM bought the processor from Intel, licensed the operating system from Microsoft — and let Microsoft license that same system to everyone else. IBM kept what it assumed was the business: designing the box and putting its name on the front. Fifteen years later anyone with a screwdriver could assemble a compatible PC, IBM was earning almost nothing on them, and the two suppliers holding the two steps IBM had handed away were among the most valuable companies on earth. **IBM did not lose on strategy or marketing. It kept the step anyone could do and gave away the two nobody else could.**

So which single step is hardest for a competitor to copy? Not the most expensive one, and not the one with the most headcount. The step that survives all three of these tests. First, **is it the customer's actual job?** Not what they said in a survey — what they are hiring you to accomplish. A person consigning a five-thousand-dollar handbag is not hiring you to "sell a bag." She is hiring you to **make a stranger believe her**. Second, **is it hard to copy?** Would a well-funded rival need years, licences, trained specialists, or accumulated data — or a weekend and a credit card? Third, **does the customer notice if it fails?** A step that fails silently earns no premium. A step that fails loudly and publicly is carrying the trust of the entire business. A step that passes all three is a chokepoint. A step that passes two is a cost centre doing an impression of a moat.

This is where the take rate becomes the most misread number in a marketplace. A take rate is not margin — it is the **top of a waterfall**, the fraction of volume that becomes revenue before a single cost is subtracted. But its *size* is informative, because a company only sustains a high take rate when it is doing something expensive on the customer's behalf. A platform charging a few percent is running a listing service; the seller is doing the work and keeping the value. A platform charging thirty or forty percent is either extracting rent or performing the chokepoint step itself — inspecting, authenticating, guaranteeing, absorbing the risk of being wrong. **The take rate does not tell you where the profit is. It tells you how much of the job the platform has taken onto its own balance sheet**, and therefore which step it has to be excellent at forever.

That value pools rather than spreads shows up well beyond individual companies. Anthropic's index of Claude usage in Canada found that **"Ontario accounts for 43.9% of conversations"** and that the four largest provinces together account for **"roughly 94% of national usage"** (McCrory, 2026, [Anthropic Research](https://www.anthropic.com/research/how-canada-uses-claude)). Adoption, like margin, concentrates. The question is never *is the activity happening* — it is *where does it settle, and who owns that place.*

Which is the argument Nick Hanauer and Jason Furman were having in August, when Hanauer charged that the system **"increasingly rewards ownership over work"** and Furman answered that **"capitalism remains the greatest engine of prosperity and innovation"** (Hanauer and Furman, 2026, [Open to Debate](https://opentodebate.substack.com/p/attend-our-live-debate-is-capitalism)). Read through a value chain, that is not a philosophical disagreement. It is a description of the chokepoint. Performing a step is work. Owning the step the whole chain depends on is ownership — and *The New York Times* found the same line running through San Francisco, where workers earning six figures said they could not keep pace with colleagues holding equity in AI companies (Martin, 2026, The New York Times, via [Techmeme](https://www.techmeme.com/260630/p10)).

What remains genuinely uncertain is the part no model resolves. **Supply in a consignment chain is borrowed, not owned** — sellers can stop supplying tomorrow, and no contract compels them. **Authentication cost rises with the quality of the fakes**, which means the defining expense grows precisely as the moat becomes more valuable, and it is never clear which curve is steeper. And **profitability in these businesses sits within a rounding error of zero**, so the same forty percent of revenue spent on gemologists and microphotography reads as an unbreakable moat in one slide deck and as obvious fat in the next.

Which leaves the question I would put to any team drawing its chain this week:

**How would you know, before the revenue told you, that the step you have been protecting is no longer the step the customer's job depends on?**

Because the chokepoint always looks like the most expensive, most annoying, most cuttable line on the whole chain. Of course it does — it is the step that is hard, and hard is expensive. There will always be someone who points at it and says *automate it, sample it, skip it for trusted sellers.* Cut a normal step and you save money. **Cut the chokepoint and you do not have a business — you have a website with other people's things on it.**
