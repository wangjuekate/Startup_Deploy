Marketing Research and the Value Chain

MENT 3235 · Module 1 · Concept Lecture · Tuesday, September 8

Case: TheRealReal FY2025 · Mental model: the Value-Chain Chokepoint

Tell this like a stand-up set. Pause where the script says pause, let them laugh, then land the point. Do not put a definition on the board in the first ten minutes.

Theater with catering

  • Marketing research is the most misunderstood phrase in this building.
  • Most of you think it means a survey. A focus group. Six people behind glass eating free sandwiches, rating a logo one through seven.
  • That is not research. That is theater with catering.

Real research answers one much ruder question

What is the customer actually paying us to do — and are we the ones doing it?

Today: how to answer that. Then where in a business the money actually collects — because it is never where you think, and almost never where the logo is.

Ask who has run a survey for a class project. Most hands go up. Do not mock them — the point is that the survey answers a question nobody needed answered.

The milkshake

  • A chain wanted to sell more milkshakes. They asked milkshake customers what would make the milkshake better. Thicker? Cheaper? More chocolate?
  • They improved it along every dimension the customers named. Sales did not move. At all.
  • So a researcher stood in the restaurant for eighteen hours and just watched when people bought them.
  • Nearly half sold before 8:30 in the morning. To people alone. In cars. Who bought nothing else, and drove off.

What they were actually buying

A long boring commute. One hand on the wheel. Not hungry yet, but will be by ten. A bagel makes crumbs. A banana is gone in twenty seconds. A donut makes your fingers greasy.

The milkshake takes twenty-three minutes through a thin straw, fits the cup holder, and makes the commute stop being boring.

Its competition was not other milkshakes. It was a banana, the radio, and a cigarette.

Beat on "eighteen hours" — the method is boring on purpose. Ask the room what the milkshake's competitor is before you reveal it.

Rule one: you are not researching your product

Ask about the thing, learn about the thing

Every question they asked the first time was about the product. Every useful answer came from the situation.

Watch a customer struggle and you learn what they are trying to get done — and that is what you are actually selling.

Now the case

A Chanel bag in a closet. Five thousand dollars. Unworn for four years. Ask “would you like to sell it?” — she says yes. Useless answer.

Watch instead: she does not know what it is worth, will not meet a stranger in a parking lot, will not photograph it and answer forty messages — and the buyer does not believe it is real.

She is not hiring anyone to sell a bag. She is hiring someone to make a stranger believe her.

This is the whole case in one line. Write it on the board and leave it there for the rest of the hour.

The most expensive handshake

  • 1980. IBM decides to build a personal computer. It wants this fast, so it buys the pieces: the processor from a small company called Intel, the operating system from a smaller one called Microsoft.
  • And critically, it lets Microsoft license that same operating system to anyone else.
  • IBM keeps the part it thought was the business: assembling the box, and putting IBM on the front.

Fifteen years later

“IBM compatible” PCs are everywhere and IBM makes almost nothing on them — anybody with a screwdriver can assemble a box. IBM eventually sold the PC business entirely.

It kept the step anyone could do, and gave away the two steps nobody else could.

IBM was not outsmarted on strategy or marketing. It was outsmarted on which step of the chain to own.

Ask which of the three pieces they would have kept in 1980. Almost everyone keeps the brand. That is the lesson.

The margin does not spread. It pools.

  • Every business is a chain of steps between a raw thing and a happy customer. Somebody sources it, verifies it, prices it, sells it, moves it, handles it coming back.
  • The margin does not spread out evenly along that chain. It collects at one or two steps and runs right past the rest.

The steps it runs past are not bad steps

Someone has to do them. They are just steps where you have no bargaining power — because if you charged more, the customer would do it themselves, or buy it from the eleven other people who do it.

Two jobs, then. Draw the chain. Find the step where the money pools.

Have them draw the chain for their own team venture before you show the figure. Six boxes, no more.

The mental model — the Value-Chain Chokepoint

Find it, own it, and starve everything else. Miss it, and you are IBM assembling boxes.

Beat 1: the chain, and that every step is necessary. Beat 2: pause here — ask which step they would cut first. Beat 3: the three tests, all three. Beat 4 is the one they will get wrong on Thursday.

Three tests — a step qualifies only if it passes all three

1 · The customer's actual job

Not what they said in the survey — what they are hiring you to accomplish. Not “sell my bag.” Make a stranger believe me.

2 · Hard to copy

Would a well-funded competitor need years, licences, trained people, accumulated data — or a weekend and a credit card?

3 · Fails loudly

If the step goes wrong, does the customer notice? A step that fails silently carries no trust, and earns no premium.

A step that passes all three is a chokepoint. A step that passes two is a cost centre pretending to be a moat.

Run their own venture through the three tests out loud. Most teams discover their “moat” fails test two.

The asymmetry that makes it a model

It always looks cuttable

The chokepoint is the most expensive, most annoying, most cuttable step on the chain. Of course it is — it is the step that is hard, and hard is expensive.

So every spreadsheet points at it and says look what this costs us. Automate it. Outsource it. Sample it. Skip it for trusted sellers.

And the other direction

The steps that are easy to grow are almost never the steps worth growing.

Buying inventory, adding advertising, opening stores — these scale beautifully in a deck because anybody can do them. That is exactly why the margin is thin there.

Cut a normal step and you save money. Cut the chokepoint and you do not have a business — you have a website with other people's things on it.

This slide is the whole reason the model is worth memorising. Say the last line slowly; it is the sentence they should hear again on Thursday.

The one question the model makes you ask

Not “will this grow revenue?” — but does this strengthen the step the whole chain depends on, or does it just add volume to a step anyone could do?

Spending forty percent of your revenue on gemologists, horologists and microphotography is not overhead. It is the entire reason a stranger will send you a five-thousand-dollar handbag through the mail.

The day it becomes “overhead” in someone's slide deck is the day the chain snaps.

Ask for one decision from their own project and run it through this question. Do not accept “it grows the business” as an answer.

The one line to carry in

Marketing research tells you what job the customer is hiring you for. The value chain tells you which step of that job you can actually get paid for.

Get the first one wrong and you build a beautiful thing nobody hires.

Get the second one wrong and you do all the work and someone else banks it.

A venture is durable only when the step you own is the same step the customer's job depends on — and it is the step hardest for anyone else to do.

Have them write this sentence down before the role pages go out. They will need it in the first five minutes of the simulation.

Next: the simulation

  • You will open the real books of TheRealReal — fourteen years spent building exactly one chokepoint.
  • It is now within twenty-four million dollars of breaking even, and is being pushed from five directions to change the thing that makes it work.
  • Six roles, four choices each. Before every decision, say which step of the chain it touches.

Your job

Not to admire the flywheel. To decide.

Find the chokepoint. Then protect it from your own team.
