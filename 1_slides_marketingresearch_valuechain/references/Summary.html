Make a Stranger Believe Her

A woman in Connecticut has a Chanel flap bag in the back of her closet. She would happily sell it. She will not mail it to a stranger who found her on the internet — and the stranger, for their part, will not wire two thousand dollars for a picture of a bag.

That deadlock is the whole business. TheRealReal has spent fourteen years building exactly one capability: the ability to make a stranger believe her.

The luxury resale market, the company writes, is “fragmented, difficult to access and laden with counterfeit goods… a vast quantity of consignable luxury goods languishes in homes, and buyers can be hesitant to purchase pre-owned luxury goods.” Languishing is the market. The company’s answer was not an app. It was approximately 1.4 million square feet of leased authentication centres in Arizona and New Jersey, staffed with “highly trained gemologists, horologists, and brand experts who collectively inspect thousands of items each day.” Every item goes through a “brand-specific authentication process.” Watches and jewelry ship with “an authentication certificate.” The policy is stated in five words: “We have a zero-tolerance policy when it comes to counterfeit goods.”

Fourteen years later, it works. In FY2025, $2,130.0 million of goods moved across the marketplace — up from $1,829.5 M and $1,726.0 M in the two years before. Sell-through ran over 80%; about 50% of products sell within 30 days of listing. Over 40 million members; 1,056 thousand active buyers placing 3,587 thousand orders at an average of $594. More than 80% of GMV came from repeat consignors and more than 80% from repeat buyers. The flywheel even runs sideways: 16% of buyers also consigned in the last twelve months and 50% of consignors also made purchases.

Here is the goal, and it is a small number. Of the $2.13 billion that moved, $692.8 million stayed — the company keeps about 32.5 cents of every GMV dollar, and warns directly that “while GMV is the primary driver of our revenue, it is not a proxy for revenue or revenue growth.” On that revenue it earned $516.8 million of gross profit — 75% — and still finished the year with a loss from operations of (23.9)million**andanetlossof**(23.9) million** and a net loss of **(41.8) million after $27.7 M of interest.

Twenty-three point nine million dollars. Against net losses of (134.2)M**theyearbeforeand**(134.2) M** the year before and **(168.5) M the year before that. The company has never been this close to breakeven, and everyone inside it can see the finish line.

Which is when the room fills with reasonable people.

The trouble is that the cheapest growth available all sits away from the buildings in Arizona and New Jersey. There is a second revenue line — direct, where the company buys the goods outright — and it is the fastest thing in the business, up 41% from $64.6 M to $91.1 million. It also cost $70.7 million to deliver. Consignment, meanwhile, produced $535.9 million of revenue at a cost of $56.6 million. Two lines, two very different dollars, and the faster one is the thinner one.

The take rate has been drifting: 37.7% in 2025, from 38.4% and 37.5%, a decline the company attributes to “sales mix into higher value items.” Consignors “achieved an overall commission rate of approximately 62% in 2025.” Marketing — $63.3 million, 9% of revenue — must buy buyers and consignors out of the same purse.

And then there is the line that dwarfs everything: operations and technology, $275.9 million — 40% of revenue. Authentication, merchandising, fulfillment, the stores, the platform. It is the largest expense in the company by a wide margin, and it is the only one that, on a spreadsheet, looks like pure overhead. Returns and cancellations run 24.2% of GMV — one dollar in four comes back after being authenticated, photographed, priced and shipped.

Technology is not separate from that step; it is that step’s cost curve. “Technology powers all aspects of our business, including our complex, individual stock keeping unit (single-SKU) inventory management system.” The company “increasingly use[s] AI in our technology platform to automate item attribution, authentication, pricing, copywriting and photo retouching,” with microphotography on high-end handbags and “proprietary item and consignor risk scoring algorithms.” Behind it sits data from “over 50 million item sales since our inception,” each item carrying “up to 50 unique attributes.”

Why this year matters more than the arithmetic suggests: the filing is candid about what happens if the verify step slips. “Our success depends on the accuracy and reliability of our authentication processes and methods… we cannot be certain that every counterfeit item will be identified.” More pointedly: “When our authentication method does not involve taking physical possession of goods prior to the sale, our ability to identify counterfeits may decrease, and order cancellations may increase.” And “as the sophistication of counterfeiters increases, it may be increasingly difficult to identify counterfeit products.” A counterfeit sale “may damage our reputation as a trusted online marketplace”; the company may face “negative press or public allegations… that our authentication processes and methods are inadequate.” Chanel has brought counterfeiting and false advertising claims, and after years of mediation the parties “were unable to reach a settlement.” The Board and Internal Audit “have identified cybersecurity as one of the risks that merits the highest level of prioritization.”

Competitors are coming for the same closet — “branded luxury goods stores, department stores, traditional brick-and-mortar consignment stores, pawn shops, auction houses, specialty retailers… as well as technology-enabled marketplaces” — and the company warns of “traditional retail competitors who expand their offerings to include resale.” It competes for consignors, it says, on “rich data and technology, and advanced authentication capabilities and expertise.”

Five people are about to propose five reasonable ways to close the gap. The woman in Connecticut is still standing at her closet, deciding whether to trust you.
