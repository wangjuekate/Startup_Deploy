The Year Tesla Won the Plug and Lost the Quarter

In 2025 every major automaker in America adopted Tesla’s charging connector.

The filing states it without ceremony: “We offer Supercharger access to non-Tesla vehicles in support of our mission, with all major automakers announcing their adoption of the North American Charging Standard (NACS) in certain markets.” One sentence. A fifteen-year argument about which plug the continent would use, settled Tesla’s way.

In the same year, revenue went down.

Total revenues $94.83 billion, a decrease of $2.86 billion. Net income attributable to common stockholders $3.79 billion, a decrease of $3.30 billion — nearly cut in half. Roughly 1.66 million vehicles produced, 1.64 million delivered. That is the shape of the year: Tesla won the standards war and had its worst automotive year in a long time, at once.

Look closer and there are two companies inside the numbers, moving in opposite directions.

Total automotive revenue fell 10% to $69,526 million. Sales alone dropped 9%, “due a decrease of approximately 8% in cash deliveries and a lower average selling price per unit driven by sales mix and higher customer incentives.” Regulatory credits — pure margin, no manufacturing — fell 28% to $1,993 M. And automotive gross margin has slid three straight years: 19.4%, 18.4%, 17.8%.

Meanwhile energy generation and storage grew 27% to $12,771 million, on 46.7 GWh deployed, “primarily due to increases in Megapack and Powerwall deployments.” Its gross margin went the other way entirely: 18.9%, 26.2%, 29.8%. Three years ago energy was the low-margin sideline; in 2025 it earns 29.8 cents on the dollar against automotive’s 17.8 — and the filing names what is pulling it: “As AI infrastructure drives rapid load growth, Megapack helps to… increase utilization of existing generation and transmission capacity.” Services and other grew 19% to $12,530 M, largely on “increases in paid Supercharging sessions.”

So: the car business is shrinking at falling margin, and the two businesses attached to it are growing at rising margin. What makes that a decision is where the money went.

Research and development rose 41%, to $6,411 million — 7% of revenue, up from 5% and 4%, “primarily due to increases in costs related to AI and other programs as we continue to expand our product roadmap and technologies.” Read the three together and the posture is unmistakable: revenue down 3%, capital expenditure down 25% ($11.34 B to $8.53 B), R&D up 41%. Tesla stopped spending on factories and started spending on intelligence. Cash and investments still rose $7.50 B to $44.06 billion, on $14.75 B of operating cash flow.

What that R&D is buying is stated outright. “In June 2025, we launched our Robotaxi service, an autonomous ride-hailing platform… along with products such as FSD (Supervised) subscriptions, unlock the potential to advance a service-driven business model based on AI, software and fleet-based profits.” And the enabling claim, quietly enormous: “Every Tesla vehicle delivered today is designed for autonomy, and we believe our capabilities and advancements in AI differentiate us from our competitors.” The fleet runs Model Ys but “in time, will include Cybercab, our purpose-built autonomous vehicle.”

That is a company arguing the dominant design is no longer the car.

The trouble is that dominance, once achieved, is not obviously worth money. Tesla now runs a charging standard for the entire industry — and monetises it partly by handing it away: “Superchargers are also available through our Supercharger for Business program, where third parties can purchase and install Superchargers and set the charging price, while Tesla remains responsible for software updates, network operations, maintenance and driver support.” Someone else owns the hardware and sets the price; Tesla keeps the software, the network and the support burden. Whether that is the winner’s position or the utility’s depends on which layer turns out to matter.

The same ambiguity runs through the competitive section. On rivals flooding into EVs the filing takes a startling line: “Overall, we believe these announcements and vehicle introductions promote the development of the electric vehicle market by highlighting the attractiveness of electric vehicles relative to internal combustion vehicles.” Competition as category-building. On the next front the tone changes: “As we seek to become a top provider of autonomous solutions, we also face competition in the fields of AI and robotics.” In energy, “the market for energy storage products and services is also highly competitive,” and in generation the rivals are “the traditional local utility companies.”

And the ground moved underneath all of it. “Certain provisions of the OBBBA, including the removal of tax credits for electric vehicles, may also impact consumer demand for electric vehicles in general.” “In 2025, governmental and regulatory actions, such as OBBBA, have restricted certain regulatory credit programs tied to our products” — which is where that 28% credit decline came from. Tariffs land unevenly: they “will have a relatively larger impact on our energy generation and storage business compared to our automotive business.” The subsidy supporting the old architecture is being withdrawn; the tariff threatening the new one is aimed at the growing half.

One more exposure. Every car improves “through over-the-air software updates” — so the channel that ships features reaches every vehicle on the road, and so does anything that gets into it. Cybersecurity risks are listed as “operational risks, intellectual property theft, fraud, extortion, harm to employees or customers and violation of data privacy or security laws”; the company runs “penetration testing using external third-party tools” and “a bug bounty program,” and “security events and data incidents are evaluated, ranked by severity and prioritized.” A fleet designed for autonomy is a fleet where a software decision is a physical one.

Tesla enters 2026 having won the argument about the plug, while the product the plug was designed for shrinks — and having bet 41% more R&D that the next argument is about who drives.
