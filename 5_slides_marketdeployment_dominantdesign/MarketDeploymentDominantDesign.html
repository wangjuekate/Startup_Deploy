Market Deployment and Dominant Design

MENT 3235 · Module 5 · Concept Lecture · Tuesday, October 20

Case: Tesla FY2025 · Mental model: the Dominant-Design Clock

The question is not “is our product better.” It is “whose design does the whole industry end up building around.” Open with the hands, not the definition.

Nobody chose USB-C

  • Hand up if your phone charges with USB-C. (most hands go up)
  • Keep it up if you chose it — if you read the spec and made an informed decision between competing charging architectures. (every hand goes down)
  • Nobody chose it. It won, and then you complied.

That is what a dominant design is

The version of a product that stops being one option and starts being the way it is done.

And once that happens, being better is no longer enough. Being different becomes a liability.

The most under-taught fact in entrepreneurship: industries do not stay open. There is a window where the design is genuinely up for grabs, then it shuts — and everything about your strategy has to change on the day it shuts. Most founders never notice the day.

Run both hand-raises for real. The second one is the lecture: adoption is not a series of choices, it is a series of compliances.

The better tape

  • Late 1970s. Two video cassette formats fight for the living room. Sony's Betamax was, by most accounts, the better technology — better picture, better engineering, and it was first.
  • JVC's VHS was, at launch, technically worse. But it recorded longer, it was cheaper to license, and JVC did what Sony refused to do: it let everybody build it.
  • The tapes in the video store were VHS. So the players people bought were VHS. So the tapes the studios pressed were VHS. So the players — you see where this goes.

Within a few years

Betamax was a trivia question. And it was still the better tape when it died.

The lesson founders hate: in a standards fight you are not competing on the product. You are competing on how many other people's decisions point at your design.

Ask which one they would have backed in 1978 knowing only the specs. Everyone backs Betamax. That is the point.

Three things decide it, and none of them is elegance

Installed base

How many units are already out there.

Complements

The tapes, the apps, the chargers, the mechanics who can fix it, the software written for it.

Switching costs

What it costs a customer to leave once they have bought in.

Sony optimised the product. JVC optimised the ecosystem. Ecosystem won — as it almost always does.

Have them name the complements for their own venture. Teams that cannot name three are usually still optimising the product.

The window shuts — and the money moves

  • Nobody tells you this about winning a standards battle: the prize is not the standard. The prize is the position the standard puts you in.
  • When a design settles, the product commoditizes. Everyone builds to the same spec, so versions converge, so price converges, so margin falls. The thing you fought for becomes the thing anybody can make.

And the money moves up a layer

After the PC architecture settled, the money left the box and went to the chip and the operating system.

After the smartphone form factor settled, it left the handset and went to the app store.

After a car becomes a car, the money starts moving to whatever is on top of the car.

Before the window shuts you ask how do I make my design win? After it shuts you ask where did the money go, and am I standing there?

Change tone here. This is the part that surprises them — the victory and the commoditization are the same event, and you say that twice today.

The move that looks insane and is not

  • Sometimes the right move is to give the standard away. Open it. Publish it. Let your competitors use it.
  • And every finance person in the room says: we spent a decade and billions building that — why are we handing it to the people trying to kill us?

Because a standard only you use is not a standard

It is a proprietary connector. And a proprietary connector on a shrinking share of the market is how you become Betamax with better marketing.

You give away the specification and you keep the position — the network, the operations, the data, the relationship, the layer above.

Everybody else builds cars that plug into your network. You get paid every time they do.

That is not generosity. It is the most aggressive move available to you — and it only works if you make it before somebody else's standard reaches critical mass.

This is the slide that decides Thursday's charging-network decision. Do not let them treat opening as a concession.

The mental model — the Dominant-Design Clock

A dominant design is not a trophy. It is a clock.

Beat 4 is the technique: ask the gate question for each product line separately. Companies have a strong instinct to run the whole firm on one strategy, and that instinct is the error.

What correct behaviour looks like at each gate

Gate 1 · Pre-dominance

Nobody knows which architecture wins. Keep bets reversible, iterate fast, do not over-commit capital to one design.

Gate 2 · The battle

One design is pulling ahead. Maximise adoption, even at the cost of exclusivity. Open it. Licence it. Subsidise complements. Margin can wait; the installed base cannot.

Gate 3 · Post-dominance

The architecture is settled. Harvest the commoditizing layer efficiently, and move your best people and capital to wherever the value went — usually one layer up.

Do this for each product line separately. That is the entire technique, and it is harder than it sounds.

Make them place two of their own products or features on the clock. Disagreement in the room is the exercise working.

The asymmetries that make it a model

Same action, opposite value

At Gate 2 you should give away the thing you would never give away at Gate 3.

Openness is a weapon while the standard is being decided, and a giveaway after it is settled. The only thing that changed was the gate.

Where incumbents die

Winning Gate 2 hands you a business that is about to commoditize. The victory and the margin compression are the same event.

Your design became the industry's design — congratulations, everyone can now build it. Read falling margins on a won product as a failure of execution and you will spend your capital defending a commodity while somebody else decides the next architecture.

The right-hand box is the Thursday argument in advance. If they accept it now, the margin slide stops looking like bad news and starts looking like evidence.

The one question the model makes you ask

Not “how do we beat this competitor?” — but which gate is this product at, and am I playing the strategy for that gate, or the one that worked at the last one?

Most companies keep playing the previous gate.

That is what “disruption” usually is — somebody winning the next gate while the incumbent is still celebrating the last one.

Ask for an example of a company still playing a previous gate. They will name one you did not expect, and it will be right.

Next: the simulation

  • You will open the real books of a company that won a standards battle — every major automaker adopted its charging connector.
  • While its car revenue fell 10%, its automotive margin slid from 19.4% to 17.8%, and it raised research spending 41% to chase an entirely different architecture that launched in June.
  • Three gates, one company, all at the same time.

Your job

Not to admire the win. To decide which gate you are playing.

Before every decision, name the gate that decision belongs to — and which product line you are naming it for.

Hand out the role pages. The Account Manager weights the leads; the argument about which gate the company is at is the argument the weighting settles.
