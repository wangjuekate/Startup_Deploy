# When the Market Stops Arguing: Dominant Design and the Window That Closes

Ask a room full of students whether their phone charges with USB-C and most hands go up. Ask who *chose* USB-C — who compared connector architectures and decided — and every hand goes down. Nobody chose it. It won, and then everyone complied. That is what a **dominant design** is: the version of a product that stops being one option among several and becomes *the way it is done*. And the moment it settles, being better stops being enough, and being different becomes a liability.

The formation is the part founders get wrong, because it is not decided on the merits. In the late 1970s two video formats fought for the living room. Sony's Betamax was, by most accounts, the better technology, and it was first. JVC's VHS was worse at launch — but it recorded longer, licensed cheaply, and JVC did the thing Sony refused to do: **it let everybody build it.** So the tapes in the rental shop were VHS, so the players people bought were VHS, so the tapes the studios pressed were VHS. Within a few years Betamax was trivia, and it was still the better tape when it died.

Three forces decide that fight and none of them is elegance. **Installed base** — how many units are already out there. **Complements** — the tapes, the apps, the chargers, the mechanics who can fix it, the software written for it. **Switching costs** — what it costs a customer to leave once they have bought in. Sony optimised the product; JVC optimised the ecosystem, and the ecosystem won, as it usually does. **In a standards fight you are not competing on the product. You are competing on how many other people's decisions point at your design.**

The implication most people miss arrives *after* the win. When a design settles, the product commoditizes: everyone builds to the same spec, versions converge, price converges, margin falls. **The victory and the margin compression are the same event.** And the money moves up a layer. After the PC architecture settled, the money left the box and went to the chip and the operating system. After the smartphone form factor settled, it left the handset for the app store. Which means the strategic question flips entirely on the day the window shuts: before, you ask *how do I make my design win?*; after, you ask *where did the money go, and am I standing there?* Read falling margins on a won product as an execution failure and you will spend your capital defending a commodity while somebody else decides the next architecture.

This is also why the move that looks insane on a spreadsheet is often correct: **at the moment the standard is being decided, give it away.** Open it, publish it, let competitors use it. Every finance person in the room will object that you spent a decade building it — but a standard only you use is not a standard, it is a proprietary connector on a shrinking share of the market. You give away the *specification* and keep the *position*: the network, the operations, the data, the layer above. That is not generosity; it is the most aggressive move available, and it only works before somebody else's design reaches critical mass. **At Gate 2 you should give away the thing you would never give away at Gate 3.** Same action, opposite value, and the only thing that changed was the gate.

You can watch a design fail to settle in real time in usage data. Anthropic's index found that **"the top 10 tasks made up 19% of all traffic in February, down from 24% in November"** — the work people bring to these systems is spreading out, not converging — while geographic concentration moved the other way, with **"the top 20 countries account for 48% of all per-capita usage, up from 45%"** (Massenkoff et al., 2026, [Anthropic Research](https://www.anthropic.com/research/economic-index-march-2026-report)). That is the signature of an industry still at Gate 1 on the product and already consolidating on distribution — which is exactly the condition in which committing hard to one architecture is most expensive and most tempting.

And standards are no longer decided only by customers. Reporting in *The New York Times* described the decision to let Nvidia sell advanced AI chips to China as reversing decades of US restrictions on adversaries (Sanger, 2025, The New York Times, via [Techmeme](https://www.techmeme.com/251210/p19)), following earlier export restrictions placed on dozens of organizations (Swanson, 2025, The New York Times, via [Techmeme](https://www.techmeme.com/250326/p15)). Whether that kind of control can decide who wins was argued at the Hopkins Forum by Will Hurd and Lindsay Gorman for, and Susan Thornton and Paul Triolo against ([Open to Debate](https://opentodebate.org/debate/the-hopkins-forum-can-the-u-s-outpace-china-in-ai-through-chip-controls/)). **When a government can add or remove installed base by policy, the standards fight acquires a player who is not buying the product.**

Which is where the honest unanswered question sits:

**In a market where the architecture is still moving and the distribution is already concentrating, how do you know which gate you are actually at — early enough for the answer to change what you do?**

Nobody has a reliable instrument for this. The window looks open right up until the moment it is shut, and the signal that it closed — competitors converging on your shape, customers no longer asking which approach you use, complements appearing that assume one design — arrives after the decisions that mattered. The practical defence is to stop running the whole company on one strategy. **Ask the gate question for each product line separately**, keep bets reversible where the architecture is unsettled, maximise adoption where it is being decided, and harvest efficiently where it is done. Most companies keep playing the previous gate, and that is what disruption usually turns out to be: somebody winning the next one while the incumbent is still celebrating the last.
