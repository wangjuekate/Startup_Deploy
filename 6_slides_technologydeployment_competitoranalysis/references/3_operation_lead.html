Case: Cerebras Systems Inc., IPO prospectus (Form 424B4, May 13, 2026)

You own one decision: how the promised capacity actually gets delivered. Everyone on your team has a different page. Yours is the supply chain. Read it, pick one option, and be ready to defend it with a number.

What the filing says

What has been promised

“In December 2025, we entered into the MRA with OpenAI, which represents a substantial portion of our projected revenues over the next several years.”

“Under the MRA with OpenAI, unless otherwise agreed to, we are required to deliver capacity tranches across specified numbers of data centers with minimum capacity thresholds upon certain time-based milestones. If we fail to deliver such capacity on the stated timelines, or if we experience a certain level of failure with respect to our service levels, OpenAI has the right to terminate a portion or all of the agreement.”

— Risk Factors

And what is attached to it

“Pursuant to the MRA with OpenAI, we received the $1.0 billion Working Capital Loan… The Working Capital Loan has a maturity date of no later than December 31, 2032, and is scheduled to be repaid in equal amortized installments over a three-year term, commencing after the delivery of the final tranche of the initial 250 MW of capacity. Interest accrues… at a rate of 6% per annum… If the MRA is terminated for any reason other than OpenAI’s material uncured breach… we may be required to immediately repay the outstanding principal balance… together with accrued interest.”

Read those two quotes together. A missed delivery date does not just cost revenue. It hands the customer a termination right and can accelerate a billion dollars.

Now look at what those tranches are built from

“We operate a fabless manufacturing model, strategically partnering with industry leaders for the production of our AI compute systems… TSMC, a leading semiconductor foundry, fabricates our cutting-edge WSEs. Advanced Semiconductor Engineering (‘ASE’) handles specialized processes, including the deposition of redistribution layers, and we manage final wafer packaging, assembly, and testing in our Sunnyvale, California facility.”

— Business

“We also use a small number of third parties to manufacture subassemblies and critical components such as printed circuit boards, I/O subsystems, cooling assemblies and power delivery modules.”

And then the sentence that matters most on this page:

“We depend on third-party suppliers, including certain sole sources, and substantially all of our manufacturing services and components are procured on a purchase order basis without capacity or volume commitments, which may harm our ability to compete with larger companies, meet customer demand…”

— Risk Factors

And the buildings they go into

“Our revenue historically has been derived from sales of our hardware systems. We are in the early stages of delivering our cloud-based offerings… which require significant data center capacity and capital investments for which we expect to require significant additional capital.”

“We lease space in or otherwise license use of third-party data centers located in the United States and Canada, and are in the process of expanding to other countries. Our business is reliant on these data center facilities. Because we lease or license use of this data center space, we do not control the operation of these third-party facilities.”

Currently those facilities are “in California, Oklahoma, and Canada.” The company has 708 employees.

Your question

You have promised dated capacity on inputs nobody has promised you. Where does the operational effort go?

Your four choices — pick one

  • (A) Convert the supply chain from purchase orders to committed capacity — wafers, sole-source components, data-centre space — because we have promised dated capacity tranches on inputs nobody has promised us.
  • (B) Keep the flexible model. Purchase orders without volume commitments are what let a company our size scale up and down with demand.
  • (C) Put the effort into manufacturing yield and throughput at Sunnyvale, where we do final packaging, assembly and test ourselves — it is the part of the chain we actually own.
  • (D) Put the effort into the data-centre build itself. Leased space we do not control is the step between a signed contract and a delivered tranche.

Before you answer

A competent lead does four things, in this order:

  1. Say which side of the switching equation your choice moves — our advantage, or the customer’s cost of leaving.
  2. Cite at least one figure from this page. Not a feeling — a number.
  3. Name what your choice gives up. Every option costs you something.
  4. State one thing that would change your answer. If nothing would, you are not reasoning.

Pick one future tranche — say the third one — and answer a single question out loud: who, specifically, has promised us the wafers for it? If the answer is “nobody, we’ll issue a purchase order,” you have found the thing you own.
