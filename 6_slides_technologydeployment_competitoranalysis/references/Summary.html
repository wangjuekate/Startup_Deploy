The Fastest Chip in the World Has Two Customers

Cerebras Systems builds a chip the size of a dinner plate.

Not figuratively. The Wafer-Scale Engine occupies 46,225 mm² of silicon — one continuous piece, where the industry cuts a wafer into hundreds of chips. It carries 4 trillion transistors, 900,000 cores and 44 GB of on-chip memory at 21 PB/s, on a 5nm TSMC process. NVIDIA’s B200 package, for comparison: 208 billion transistors, about 1,600 mm². The Cerebras part is “58 times larger… and has 2,625 times more memory bandwidth.”

Nobody built this before because it could not be built. “To build the WSE, we solved the 75-year-old compute industry problem of wafer-scale integration.”

The claim that follows is the company’s whole reason to exist: “We are building the fastest AI infrastructure in the world.” “Cerebras Inference delivers answers up to 15 times faster than leading GPU-based solutions,” and many customers have achieved “more than 10 times faster training time-to-solution.” The software removes the last excuse: “Cerebras Compiler compiles PyTorch models directly to the WSE, eliminating the need for CUDA or distributed programming,” so customers “can scale training and inference across frontier-size models without rewriting code.”

And the lock-in argument is genuinely elegant: “Once customers adopt fast inference, user expectations for interactivity rise, and engineering teams shift from latency optimizations to other work, making it difficult to return to slower inference.” Speed, once tasted, is load-bearing.

The revenue looks like the technology is winning: $24.6 M in 2022 → $78.7 M in 2023 → $290.3 M in 2024 → $510.0 million in 2025, the last year up 76%.

Now read the same page more carefully, and a different company appears.

“G42 accounted for 24.0% and 85.0% of our total revenue for the years ended December 31, 2025 and 2024, respectively, and in the year ended December 31, 2025, Mohamed bin Zayed University of Artificial Intelligence (‘MBZUAI’) accounted for 62.0% of our total revenue.” Two customers, 86% of the year — and they “are considered related parties with respect to each other.” Worse: “As of December 31, 2025, one customer (MBZUAI) accounted for 77.9% of our accounts receivable balance.” A year earlier G42 was 91.0%.

The fastest AI infrastructure in the world is, commercially, one relationship.

The profit line tells its own story. Cerebras reports net income of $237.8 million — its first. But the loss from operations widened to $(145.9) million, and the profit arrives entirely through $390.7 million of other income. Non-GAAP, the company reports a net loss of $75.7 million. The reported profit is an accounting event, not an operating one.

Underneath, the mix is moving the wrong way. Gross margin fell from 42.3% to 39.0% — hardware carries about 42.9%, cloud and other services about 29.9%, and cloud is the faster-growing line, up 94% to $151.6 M against hardware’s $358.4 M. Growth is arriving in the line that keeps least. R&D is $243.3 million — 48% of revenue; sales and marketing more than tripled to $70.6 M. 708 employees carry all of it.

So the goal for 2026 is to escape two customers without losing the technical lead that is the only reason anyone would switch. A door has opened.

“OpenAI, the world’s leading foundation model lab, selected us to be its fast inference solution.” In December 2025 the two signed a Master Revenue Agreement “which represents a substantial portion of our projected revenues over the next several years.” AWS “has signed a binding term sheet with us to become the first hyperscaler to deploy Cerebras in its own data centers.” The base is expanding too: “our top ten customers… increased their aggregate spend with us by approximately 80% within 12 months.” The market is “$251 billion in 2025… expected to grow to $672 billion by 2029—a 28% CAGR,” with “AI inference… grow[ing] more than twice as fast as AI training infrastructure.”

Read the OpenAI terms, though. Cerebras must “deliver capacity tranches across specified numbers of data centers with minimum capacity thresholds upon certain time-based milestones. If we fail to deliver such capacity on the stated timelines… OpenAI has the right to terminate a portion or all of the agreement.” Against that it took a $1.0 billion Working Capital Loan at 6%, repayable after the final tranche is delivered — and immediately if the agreement terminates. The escape from customer concentration is financed by, and conditioned on, a delivery schedule.

Which lands on a supply chain the company does not own. It runs “a fabless manufacturing model” — TSMC fabricates the wafers, ASE handles specialized processes, final packaging happens in Sunnyvale. It depends on “third-party suppliers, including certain sole sources,” and substantially all manufacturing is procured “on a purchase order basis without capacity or volume commitments.” No commitments in either direction. The cloud business “require[s] significant data center capacity and capital investments” — in leased facilities the company “do[es] not control.”

It also sells advanced compute across borders. “Our offerings are subject to U.S. export controls… We cannot guarantee that we will be successful in obtaining all required licenses in the future,” under rules “BIS has changed and may again change… at any time.” And: “We identified material weaknesses in our internal control over financial reporting.” The leased data centers are “vulnerable to damage or interruption… including computer and other cybersecurity vulnerabilities, physical or electronic break-ins, human error, malfeasance or interference.”

The competitors have none of these problems. “Our hardware primarily competes against solutions from NVIDIA Corporation, Advanced Micro Devices, Inc., Intel Corporation” and hyperscaler accelerators, plus AWS, Azure, Google Cloud, Oracle and CoreWeave. “Some of our competitors have greater marketing, financial, distribution, and manufacturing resources than we do.”

Fifteen times faster is a real advantage. The question is whether it is a business.
