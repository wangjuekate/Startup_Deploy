Technology Deployment and Competitor Analysis

MENT 3235 · Module 6 · Concept Lecture · Tuesday, October 27

Case: Cerebras Systems · Mental model: the 10× Threshold vs the Ecosystem Moat

Last week: whose design does the industry build around. This week: you have the better technology and the incumbent still might win. Say that framing first.

“If I built a better version of that, I'd win”

  • Every one of you has had that thought.
  • I want to spend an hour ruining that sentence — not because it is wrong, but because it is missing about four words, and those four words are where all the money is.

The question you should be able to answer by the end

How much better do you have to be to make someone change?

Not “is it better.” Not “did the benchmark improve.” How much, and better at what, and better than what they would have to give up.

Do not answer the question yet. Everything until the figure is evidence for one number they will not guess.

The keyboard you are using wrong

  • Look at your laptop. Q-W-E-R-T-Y. Designed in the 1870s for a mechanical typewriter — the standard story is the letters were arranged to slow typists down so the metal arms would not jam.
  • There is no typewriter. The arms are gone. The problem it solved has not existed in your lifetime. And every one of you is still typing on it.
  • In the 1930s, Dvorak patented a layout he claimed was substantially faster and less tiring. Whether or not the studies hold up, notice what happened: it did not matter.

Why it did not matter

You would retrain your fingers for weeks. Every computer you ever sit down at would have the wrong layout. Every keyboard on sale would still be QWERTY. Every person you asked for help would be useless to you.

That is not a product comparison. That is a switching cost.

First real rule of competitor analysis: your competitor is not the other product. Your competitor is the customer's cost of leaving what they have — and that cost usually has nothing to do with the product itself.

Ask how many have tried Dvorak. Usually one person, who quit. That anecdote does more work than the rule does.

The incumbent's moat is made of other people's work

The complements

Everything built to work with it. Software, add-ons, spare parts, integrations, plugins.

The skills

Every person hired who knows how to use it — and every course, tutorial and forum answer written about it.

The habits

The workflows, the checklists, the way the team already does it.

The risk of being wrong

“Nobody ever got fired for buying the incumbent.” If the safe choice fails it is bad luck. If your choice fails it is bad judgement — and it is their fault.

None of that was built by the incumbent. It was built by customers, partners and third parties, for free, over a decade. You can out-engineer a company. You cannot out-engineer ten years of everybody else's accumulated effort.

Which is why the incumbent is often not even trying to win on your dimension. They do not have to be as good as you. They have to be good enough that switching is not worth it.

Slow down here; this is the important part. Ask them to name the moat for a product they use daily — the fourth card is the one that never comes up unprompted.

So how much better do you have to be?

2% better

A rounding error against retraining a team.

15% better

A slide in a sales deck.

10× better

A business case that survives contact with a procurement department.

And the part technical founders stop reading before

Ten times better on the wrong dimension is worth nothing. Ten times faster is decisive only if the customer is waiting. Ten times cheaper is decisive only if they are cost-constrained.

If the buyer's actual pain is “my engineers know the other thing,” then your 15× benchmark and their retraining budget are having two completely different conversations.

The 10× rule of thumb comes from the Grove era at Intel. Give them the number, then immediately take it away with the wrong-dimension caveat.

The mental model — the 10× Threshold vs the Moat

It is a subtraction — and both sides of it are your job.

Beat 3 is where the room usually stops nodding. Beat 4 is the move almost nobody thinks of as strategy; beat 5 is why time is not on your side.

Two moves — and most founders only know one

Grow your side

Raise the advantage. Engineering. Be ten times better on the dimension they actually buy.

This is the move every technical team already knows, and the only one most of them ever make.

Shrink their side

Lower the cost of leaving. Compatibility. Speak their language, take their file formats, run their code unchanged, plug into the channels they already buy through, sell the first unit as an experiment instead of a migration.

Every hour spent making the switch smaller is worth as much as an hour spent making your product better — and it usually costs less.

Ask for the cheapest thing their own venture could do to make a customer's switch smaller. The answers are usually a day of work and nobody had scheduled them.

The asymmetries that make it a model

The gap grows while you build

Your advantage has to be proven once. Their moat gets rebuilt every day, by people who do not work for them.

Every new graduate who learns the incumbent's tool, every tutorial written, every integration shipped by a third party makes the moat deeper — and none of it costs the incumbent anything.

What actually compounds

A technical lead is temporary. A distribution position is compounding.

Win on speed and they copy the speed in two years. Win a place inside the channel where everybody already buys — the marketplace, the hyperscaler, the standard toolchain — and you collect customers while you sleep. The deal that makes you easy to buy is worth more than the benchmark that makes you better.

This pair is the whole argument for the partnership decision on Thursday. Get them to say it before the numbers do.

The one question the model makes you ask

Not “are we better?” — but on the dimension this buyer is actually bleeding on, is our advantage bigger than everything they would have to throw away — and what is the cheapest thing we could do to make that pile smaller?

You do not win by being better. You win when your advantage on the dimension they care about is bigger than everything they would have to abandon to come and get it.

You can make your side bigger — that is engineering. You can also make their side smaller — and that is usually the cheaper, faster, more decisive move.

Force the second half of the question. Teams answer the first half fluently and have never once costed the second.

Next: the simulation

  • You will open the real books of a company that built a chip 58 times larger than its competitor's, runs models 15 times faster, and spends 48% of its revenue on research.
  • And last year took 86% of its revenue from two related customers — while losing money on operations.
  • They have the technology. The question on the table is whether they can get anyone to switch before the moat gets deeper.

Your job

Not to admire the silicon. To decide.

For every decision, say which side of the subtraction it changes — yours, or theirs.

Customer concentration is a competitive fact, not just a finance risk. Flag it here so the Financial Lead and the Marketing Lead arrive already disagreeing.
