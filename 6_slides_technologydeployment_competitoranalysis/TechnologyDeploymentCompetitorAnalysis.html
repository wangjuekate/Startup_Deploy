Deploying Technology Against Competitors

MENT 3235 · Module 6 · Concept Lecture · Tuesday, October 27

Why would a customer choose your venture over what they already use?

1. Study: what are you competing against?

  • Direct competitor: another product serving the same customer and need.
  • Indirect alternative: a different way to get the same outcome.
  • Status quo: a manual workaround or choosing to do nothing.

QueueLess: lunch between classes

A campus ordering app competes with restaurant ordering sites, walk-up counters, and bringing lunch from home. The customer wants a meal that fits a short break.

Ask students to name one direct competitor, one indirect alternative, and the current workaround. Define the job before narrowing the competitor set. Bringing lunch matters even though it has no ordering technology.

Have students select two alternatives and the status quo. Study strengths as carefully as weaknesses. A competitor may rationally serve another segment; a missing feature is not necessarily a mistake.

Build an evidence record before a feature list

  • Try the journey: sign up, order, pay, get support, and attempt to leave.
  • Read public evidence: pricing, documentation, reviews, and product demonstrations.
  • Watch and interview customers: ask about the last real incident, not whether they like your idea.

Record each claim

Observation → source/date → interpretation → uncertainty → next check.

A review about a missed pickup is a clue. Observe the workflow and interview other users before calling it a segment-wide pain.

Prompt: “Tell me about the last time lunch made you late.” Ask to see receipts or workflow artifacts with consent. Reviews can overrepresent extreme experiences. Keep competitor marketing claims separate from observed outcomes; triangulate important claims.

Choose axes from customer evidence. An empty area is an opportunity only if customers want it and you can serve it economically.

Ask students to defend the axes before placing companies. Define cost consistently, including fees, and measure predictability with actual versus promised timing. Positions differ by segment and context. This map is not measured market data; students must replace hypotheses with evidence.

2. Discover pain in the customer’s workflow

MomentObserveAsk about a real event
Before orderingSearching, comparing, planning“How did you decide you had enough time?”
During pickupWaiting, checking status, asking staff“What happened when the order was not ready?”
After pickupMissed commitments, refunds, workarounds“What did that delay cost you? What did you do next?”

From complaint to pain hypothesis

“The app is annoying” is vague. Students with short class breaks cannot reliably collect lunch within the time available is a specific hypothesis to investigate.

Do not infer all customer pain from competitor feature gaps. Observe both students and restaurant staff; improving a student screen will fail if kitchen capacity is the actual constraint. Capture current workarounds and consequences, not just requested features.

Prioritize pain before choosing technology

  • Frequency: how often does this happen for this segment?
  • Severity: what time, money, or opportunity does it cost?
  • Workaround: what effort do customers already spend avoiding it?
  • Readiness: will they try a different workflow or pay for relief?

Decision gate

Select one pain with repeated evidence and a reachable customer group.

If the evidence is weak, conduct more observation or narrow the segment. Do not turn an untested complaint into a product roadmap.

Ask students to compare unpredictable pickup with limited menu variety. Which pain matters more for a ten-minute break? Avoid arbitrary numerical scores that conceal weak evidence. An observed workaround or a real pilot commitment is stronger evidence than hypothetical enthusiasm.

3. Position your venture around a specific gap

A positioning statement

For [customer in a situation] who struggles with [validated pain], our venture delivers [outcome] compared with [current alternative], because [credible mechanism and evidence].

QueueLess: a positioning hypothesis

For students with short lunch breaks, QueueLess aims to make pickup timing dependable compared with ordering ahead without a dependable pickup window. Capacity-based slots and readiness updates are the proposed mechanism; a pilot must establish the benefit.

Read the statement aloud without naming the technology first. Ask what evidence would overturn it. Students who bring lunch might not be the initial segment; target those already buying meals and experiencing unpredictable pickup. A proposed mechanism is not yet proof.

Keep the useful capability or convention; do not assume you need every competitor feature.

Ask students to identify one competitor strength they should preserve and one existing venture assumption they should retain. “Keep” means maintain customer value, not copy proprietary assets. Check feasibility and staff workload before promising compatibility.

These are conditional decisions for the fictional example, not established findings. For each row ask what evidence supports it and what would reverse the decision. A feature should not survive simply because the team already built it.

Choose technology that supports the position

Trace the decision

Study: alternatives already support ordering.
Pain: pickup timing may be unreliable.
Keep: menu, payment, and order intake.
Change: how pickup commitments are made.

Deploy the smallest useful capability

Technology: capacity-based pickup slots and readiness updates.
Start: a simple staff capacity setting before complex forecasting.
Measure: actual readiness against the promised window.

If the bottleneck is staffing or kitchen operations, software alone will not solve it.

Ask why this positioning calls for capacity management rather than recommendations or a chatbot. Treat technology as a means of delivering the chosen outcome. Validate the workflow manually before investing in automation; preserve the same customer promise across both.

5. Deploy narrowly and test against the alternative

Pilot choiceQueueLess example — illustrative targets
Initial deploymentOne restaurant, one lunch period, opt-in users; retain the existing ordering route
Baseline and outcomeCompare matched lunch periods: ready-on-time rate and pickup wait
Success criteria set in advanceAt least 90% ready within the promised window; median wait at least 30% below baseline
Adoption and operating checksAt least half of pilot users return within two weeks; staff effort and errors do not worsen
DecisionExpand only if outcome and adoption criteria hold; otherwise revise and retest

Make switching manageable: familiar payment, minimal training, clear support, and an easy fallback.

Targets are classroom examples, not universal thresholds. Define “ready,” record timestamps consistently, and compare similar order volumes and meal types. Track restaurant workload and economics as well as student outcomes. A small pilot is directional evidence; inspect failures and avoid claiming causality from an uncontrolled comparison. Stop or revise if operations deteriorate.

The mental model: evidence → choice → deployment

Study and discovery lead the process. Technology follows a clear competitive position.

Recap each step through QueueLess: study ordering sites, counters, and packed lunches; investigate unreliable pickup; target students with short breaks; keep ordering conventions while changing pickup commitments; test capacity-based slots. Reveal the feedback loop last. If the pilot fails, revisit the pain or position rather than automatically adding features.

The final takeaway

Preserve what customers value.
Change what causes meaningful pain.
Prove the improvement is worth switching for.

Before you build, complete this sentence

Compared with this alternative, for this customer, we will keep these valued strengths and change this source of pain. We will know it works when this outcome improves and customers adopt it.

Ask one student to complete the sentence using their own venture. Challenge technology claims without customer outcomes and competitor claims without evidence. The takeaway should include both what stays familiar and what changes.
