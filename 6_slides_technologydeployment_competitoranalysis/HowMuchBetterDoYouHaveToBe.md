# How Much Better Do You Have to Be? Competitor Analysis for People With the Better Technology

Everyone has had the thought: *if I built a better version of that, I would win.* That sentence is missing four words, and those four words are where the money is. The question is not whether your technology is better. It is **how much better you have to be to make somebody change** — better at what, and better than what they would give up.

Look at the keyboard in front of you. QWERTY was arranged in the 1870s around the mechanics of a typewriter whose arms could jam. There is no typewriter; the problem it solved has not existed in your lifetime. In the 1930s Dvorak patented a layout claimed to be faster and less tiring, and whether or not the studies hold, notice what happened: **it did not matter.** To switch you would retrain your fingers for weeks, every machine you sat down at would have the wrong layout, and every person you asked for help would be useless to you. That is not a product comparison. **That is a switching cost — and your real competitor is not the other product, it is the customer's cost of leaving what they have.**

A standard competitor analysis — five forces, rivals, suppliers, buyers, entrants, substitutes — maps the industry correctly and still under-weights this, because the decisive quantity sits on nobody's org chart. When a customer has used the incumbent for ten years, what they would abandon is: the **complements** built to work with it, the **skills** of every person they hired who knows it, the **habits and processes** their team runs on, and the **career risk** of being the one who chose differently. Nobody was ever fired for buying the incumbent; if the safe choice fails it is bad luck, and if yours fails it is bad judgement. Underline this: **none of that was built by the incumbent.** Customers, partners and third parties built it, for free, over a decade. You can out-engineer a company; you cannot out-engineer ten years of everybody else's accumulated effort. Which is why the incumbent is often not trying to be as good as you. **They only have to be good enough that switching is not worth it.**

Hence the rule of thumb from Andy Grove's era at Intel: a new technology must be roughly **an order of magnitude better on the dimension the buyer actually buys.** Two percent better is a rounding error against retraining a team. Fifteen percent is a slide in a sales deck. Ten times is a business case that survives procurement. But — and this is where technical founders stop reading too early — **ten times better on the wrong dimension is worth nothing.** Ten times faster is decisive only if the customer is waiting. Ten times cheaper is decisive only if they are cost-constrained. If the buyer's actual pain is *my engineers know the other thing*, your benchmark and their retraining budget are having two different conversations.

How expensive is that retraining, really? Anthropic and an independent researcher reviewed the evidence, drawing on **"56 randomized US studies"**, and found that job training programs **"produce positive but modest effects: for each person offered a training slot, employment rises by two to three percentage points and earnings by roughly $1,000 a year, against a cost of about $13,000"** (Roodman & Massenkoff, 2026, [Anthropic Research](https://www.anthropic.com/research/reviewing-the-evidence-on-worker-retraining-programs)). The programs that worked several times better were the ones that **"partner with employers in a high-demand industry and place people directly into jobs in that field."** Read that as a competitor-analysis finding, not a labour-policy one: **retraining humans is expensive and slow, and works best when someone removes the friction rather than exhorting people to cross it.** That is exactly what you must do for a buyer's engineers.

Worse, the gap you must clear is not fixed. It grows while you build, because the moat is rebuilt daily by people who do not work for the incumbent — every graduate who learns their tool, every tutorial written, every integration shipped. The compounding shows up even in usage data: **"long-tenure users are about 5 percentage points more likely to have a successful conversation"** than newer ones (Massenkoff et al., 2026, [Anthropic Research](https://www.anthropic.com/research/economic-index-march-2026-report)). Familiarity is not a soft factor. It is measurable performance that a challenger's customer would be giving up on day one.

So there are two moves, and most founders know only one. **Grow your side** — be ten times better on the dimension they buy; that is engineering. Or **shrink their side** — take their file formats, run their code unchanged, plug into the channels they already buy through, sell the first unit as an experiment rather than a migration. The second is usually cheaper, faster and more decisive, and almost nobody calls it strategy. It is also why **a technical lead is temporary and a distribution position is compounding**: speed can be copied in two years, but a place inside the channel where everyone already buys keeps collecting customers while you sleep.

None of this is purely a market question any more. Whether policy can decide a technology race was argued at the Hopkins Forum by Will Hurd and Lindsay Gorman for chip controls and Susan Thornton and Paul Triolo against ([Open to Debate](https://opentodebate.org/debate/the-hopkins-forum-can-the-u-s-outpace-china-in-ai-through-chip-controls/)), while *The New York Times* reported the US adding export restrictions on 80 organizations, including customers of the major chipmakers (Swanson, 2025, The New York Times, via [Techmeme](https://www.techmeme.com/250326/p15)). A rule can shrink or enlarge a moat overnight without a single customer changing their mind.

Which leaves the question to bring to any competitor analysis:

**On the dimension this buyer is actually bleeding on, is our advantage bigger than everything they would have to throw away — and what is the cheapest thing we could do to make that pile smaller?**

You do not win by being better. You win when your advantage on the dimension they care about exceeds what they must abandon to come and get it. That is a subtraction, and **both sides of it are your job.**
