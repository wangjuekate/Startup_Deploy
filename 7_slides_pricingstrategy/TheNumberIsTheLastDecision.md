# The Number Is the Last Decision: Pricing Strategy, Elasticity, and What You Charge For

Give me a price for a cup of coffee. Four dollars? Fine — four dollars **per what?** Per cup, per ounce, per visit, per month unlimited, per hour in the chair? Four dollars a cup and four dollars an hour are the same number and two completely different businesses: different customers, different behaviour, different furniture. **Pricing is three decisions, and the number is the last of them and the least interesting.** First the **metric** — what you charge for. Then the **level** — how much. Then the **fences** — who pays more, and why they cannot simply buy the cheap version.

Start with the metric, because it is the strategy. A printer costs eighty dollars and the manufacturer may make nothing on it; then the cartridges cost sixty dollars each, forever. Look at what that decides: who buys, who is profitable (heavy printers), what the company must defend at all costs (the cartridge), and therefore what R&D gets funded. They did not pick a number. **They picked a unit.** Flip it: a gym charging thirty dollars a month for unlimited visits has decided its best customer is **the person who never comes.**

The test is short: **charge for something that gets bigger when the customer is getting more value.** If your customer is delighted, successful and growing and your revenue did not move, the unit is wrong. If they are barely using you and paying a fortune, it is wrong the other way, and they will leave the moment somebody offers better. Get the metric right and revenue grows without a salesperson touching it.

**Price elasticity** is the second decision's discipline. It measures how much quantity moves when price moves — the percentage change in units sold divided by the percentage change in price. Demand is **elastic** when a small price rise costs a disproportionate amount of volume — plentiful substitutes, discretionary purchase, an easy switch — and **inelastic** when volume barely reacts: no alternative, high switching cost, a purchase small relative to the value it protects. So a price rise is only profitable when demand is inelastic *at that point on the curve* — and elasticity is not a property of your product but of your product **in a particular customer's situation.** The same software is elastic for a hobbyist and inelastic for the enterprise that built its quarterly close around it. That difference is exactly what fences exist to capture: editions, tiers, support levels, volume commitments, regions. **A single price for everyone is not fairness. It is money left on both ends.**

Timing is part of the same picture. Anthropic's index of when people use Claude found that **"recipe requests are 2.3 times more frequent at 6 p.m. compared to the average"** and that **"on April 14, tax-related clusters were eight times as common as on the average day in May"** (Massenkoff et al., 2026, [Anthropic Research](https://www.anthropic.com/research/economic-index-june-2026-report)). Demand is not a flat line you attach a price to. It has hours, seasons and deadlines, and willingness to pay peaks with urgency. Price as though every unit of demand were identical and you are quietly subsidising your most desperate customers.

Then the trap, and it is the one that catches good companies. **If you charge for usage, every efficiency improvement you ship is a tax on yourself.** Your engineers make the system twice as fast; it now takes half the resources to do the same job; you have delivered a genuine competitive improvement and cut your own revenue on every existing workload. Sometimes the freed budget returns as new work — that is most of the history of computing — but it is not automatic or fast, and in the quarter before it happens your revenue is simply down. Meanwhile the customer employs a team whose whole job is optimising spend, and under consumption pricing they can shrink your revenue **without cancelling anything**: no renewal conversation, no churn event, no signal.

That is the price of a beautiful pricing model. **A usage metric aligns you with your customer's success and with your customer's efficiency, and those are not the same thing.** You signed up for both when you picked the metric — which is why the asymmetry matters more than the arithmetic: **the level is easy to change and cheap to get wrong; the metric is nearly impossible to change and it decides your entire company.** You can raise prices next quarter. You cannot move from per-seat to per-usage without renegotiating every contract, rebuilding billing, and frightening every customer whose budget was built on the old shape.

There is also a limit on how much of this is yours to decide. Open to Debate's Hopkins Forum on breaking up big tech put Bharat Ramamurti and Matt Stoller against Jennifer Huddleston and Geoffrey Manne ([Open to Debate](https://opentodebate.substack.com/p/the-hopkins-forum-tech-titans-or)) — an argument about who sets prices when a platform sits between everyone. And costs keep moving underneath: *The New York Times* described a two-employee company reaching $401 million in sales on AI tools costing a few hundred dollars a month (Griffith, 2026, The New York Times, via [Techmeme](https://www.techmeme.com/260402/p12)). When a competitor's cost to serve collapses, your level is repriced whether or not you touch it.

Which leaves the question I have not seen anyone answer cleanly:

**When the cost of delivering your product is falling fast and unevenly, how do you set a price that reflects the value you deliver rather than the cost you happen to incur this year?**

Cost-plus pricing dies first there, because the cost is a moving target and your customer reads the same headlines you do. Value-based pricing survives longer, but only if you can *name* the value in the customer's units well enough to defend it at renewal. And every consumption metric quietly bets that customers will use more of a thing that keeps getting cheaper. So ask the question that keeps the metric honest: **when our customer succeeds, does the number on our invoice go up on its own — and what happens to that number on the day they get good at using us?**
