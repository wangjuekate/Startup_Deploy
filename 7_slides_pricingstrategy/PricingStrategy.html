Pricing Strategy

MENT 3235 · Module 7 · Concept Lecture · Tuesday, November 3

Case: Snowflake FY2026 · Mental model: the Value-Metric Triangle

Everyone thinks pricing is a number. It is three decisions, and the number is the least interesting one. Open by taking a price from the room — do not skip it.

Four dollars per what?

  • Somebody give me a price for a cup of coffee. (they say four dollars)
  • Four dollars per what? Per cup? Per ounce? Per visit? Per month, unlimited? Per hour in the chair? Per person at the table?
  • Four dollars a cup and four dollars an hour are the same number and two completely different businesses — different customers, different behaviour, different furniture, different everything.

What to leave with today

The number is the last decision you make and the least important one.

What actually determines whether you have a business is what you charge for and who pays more. Almost nobody teaches that, and almost every founder gets it wrong on their first venture.

Take a real answer and use their number for the rest of the slide. The unit question lands much harder when it is their four dollars.

The printer that hates you

  • A printer costs eighty dollars. The manufacturer might make nothing on it — might even lose money.
  • Then the cartridges cost sixty dollars each, forever, and at the volumes involved that ink has famously been compared to some of the most expensive liquids on earth.
  • Everybody in this room has been angry about this at least once.

But look at what that pricing decides

Who buys — people who need a printer today and do not do the arithmetic. Who is profitable — heavy printers. What the company must protect — the cartridge, which is why third-party cartridges are fought so hard. What R&D gets funded.

They did not pick a number. They picked a unit. Eighty for the box and sixty for the ink is a completely different firm from four hundred for the box and ten for the ink — same revenue per customer, entirely different business.

Then flip it: a gym at thirty dollars a month, unlimited visits, decides that its best customer is the person who never comes. The unit chose the customer, and the customer chose the business.

Rule one: the unit is a strategy, not an invoice line

Charge for something that gets bigger when the customer is getting more value.

If your customer is delighted and successful and your revenue did not move, your unit is wrong.

If your customer is barely using you and paying a fortune, your unit is wrong in the other direction — and they will leave the moment somebody offers them a better one.

Get it right and you have a business that grows without a salesperson: the customer succeeds, the meter runs, revenue goes up, nobody had to negotiate anything.

This is the part that makes the difference in their team projects. Make each team state their metric out loud and test it against the sentence.

The trap: efficiency becomes a tax on yourself

  • If you charge for usage, then every improvement that makes your product more efficient makes your customer use less of it.
  • Your engineers make the system twice as fast. It now takes half the resources to do the same job. You have delivered a genuine, valuable improvement — and cut your own revenue in half on every existing workload.
  • Does the freed budget come back as new work? Sometimes, spectacularly — that is the entire history of computing. But it is not automatic, and it is not fast.

And it is somebody's job to shrink you

Your customer has a whole department whose job is “optimize our spend.” They are literally paid to make your revenue smaller.

Under consumption pricing they can do it without cancelling anything — no renewal conversation, no churn event, no signal. Your revenue just quietly thins.

This is the price of a beautiful pricing model, and it is real. Charge for value delivered and you are aligned with the customer perfectly — including on the days when the aligned answer is “use less of you.”

Sit in the silence after “cut your own revenue in half.” This is the whole case they open on Thursday.

The mental model — the Value-Metric Triangle

In this order, because the order matters. Get the metric right and the level almost sets itself.

Beat 1 and its test are the lecture. Beats 2 and 3 are quick. Beat 4 is what they should be able to recite on Thursday.

How to read it — in order

1 · The metric

What one thing goes on the invoice? Test it: does this number get bigger when the customer is getting more value? If yes, a growth engine. If no, a negotiation every renewal.

2 · The level

Only now pick the price per unit. Bounded by the customer's alternative and your cost to serve — and the easiest of the three to change later.

3 · The fences

Different customers get different value from the same thing. Editions, tiers, support levels, volume commitments, regions. A single price for everyone is not fairness — it is money left on both ends.

Fences let the customers who get more pay more, without losing the ones who get less.

Most teams have a metric and a level and no fences at all. Ask each team who their cheap customer is; the silence is the finding.

The asymmetries that make it a model

Spend your thinking where it is expensive to be wrong

The level is easy to change and cheap to get wrong. The metric is almost impossible to change and it decides your entire company.

You can raise prices next quarter. You cannot switch from per-seat to per-usage without renegotiating every contract, retraining every salesperson, rebuilding billing, and terrifying every customer whose budget was built on the old shape.

You bought both halves

A usage metric aligns you with your customer's success — and with your customer's efficiency. Those are not the same thing.

When the customer grows, you grow. When the customer optimizes, you shrink. You signed up for both when you picked the metric, and you cannot have only the first half.

The right-hand box is the case. Say it in these words now so that Thursday's disclosure reads as a consequence rather than a surprise.

The one question the model makes you ask

Not “what should we charge?” — but when our customer succeeds, does the number on our invoice go up on its own — and what happens to that number on the day they get good at using us?

Pricing is not a number. It is three decisions, in this order: the metric, the level, the fences.

Get the metric right and the level almost sets itself. Get the metric wrong and no level saves you.

Have each team answer the second half of the question about their own venture. Nobody has ever thought about the day the customer gets good at using them.

Next: the simulation

  • You will open the real books of a company that charges purely for consumption and grew revenue 29% to $4.68 billion.
  • It keeps 125 cents of every dollar its existing customers spent last year — down from 133 two years ago.
  • And it states plainly, in its own annual report, that making its product more efficient may reduce its revenue.

Your job

Not to admire the model. To decide.

For every decision, say which corner of the triangle it moves — and whether you could ever move it back.
