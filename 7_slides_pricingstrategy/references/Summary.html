The Company That Gets Paid When You Wait

Snowflake sells neither licences nor seats. It sells time on machines.

“We generate the substantial majority of our revenue from fees charged to our customers based on the compute, storage, and data transfer resources consumed on our platform.” Customers select those resources “separately, at their discretion.” The pitch is fairness: the platform is “priced based on a consumption-based business model, reducing hidden costs and helping ensure that customers pay only for what they use.”

A beautiful model with a knife in it. Buried in the risk factors is the sentence that should stop any pricing student cold:

“New software releases or hardware improvements… may make our platform more efficient, enabling customers to consume fewer compute, storage, and data transfer resources to accomplish the same workloads… To the extent these improvements do not result in an offsetting increase in new workloads, we may experience lower revenue.”

Make the product better and the bill goes down. Every engineer who shaves a query from twelve seconds to four has just cut an invoice. That is the trade-off the whole company sits on, and in fiscal 2026 — the year ended January 31, 2026 — it stopped being theoretical.

The top line still looks strong. Revenue $4,683.9 million, up from $3,626.4 M and $2,806.5 M; product revenue $4,472.3 million; gross profit $3,146.1 million — 67%, steady. 13,328 total customers, up from 10,996; 733 customers now spend more than $1 million a year, up from 576 and 449; 790 Forbes Global 2000 customers contributing about 43% of revenue. Remaining performance obligations $9,771.5 million. Free cash flow $1,120.3 million.

Now the number that matters most in a consumption business. Net revenue retention — what last year’s customers spent this year — has gone 133% → 126% → 125%. Still above 100, so customers are still growing. But the expansion engine is decelerating, three years running.

And underneath the strong revenue is a company that does not make money. Operating loss (1,435.2)million—31(1,435.2) million — 31% of revenue.** Net loss **(1,331.6) million. The largest single reason is not compute: stock-based compensation of $1,599.5 million — 34% of revenue, which alone exceeds the operating loss. It is improving as a share (41%, 42% prior), but $3.1 billion of unvested awards remain, over a weighted-average 2.7 years. Sales and marketing takes $2,062.1 million (44%) and R&D $1,969.5 million (42%). G&A carries $108.7 million of asset impairment, mostly the cease-use of the San Mateo office.

So: three years of falling retention, a 31%-of-revenue operating loss, and a pricing model whose own filing warns that efficiency reduces revenue.

The pressure comes from the customers, and it is deliberate. “Many customers are attempting to rationalize budgets, prioritize cash flow management, and optimize consumption amidst macroeconomic uncertainty. In any given period, there is a risk that customer consumption of our platform will be slower than we expect.” Specifically, they “prioritize cash flow management, including through shortened contract duration, and optimize consumption, including by reducing storage through shorter data retention policies.” Customers have learned to read the meter.

The contract structure amplifies it. Customers “typically enter into capacity arrangements with a term of one to four years, or consume our platform under on-demand arrangements.” Consumption “accelerates… and often exceeds their initial capacity commitment amounts” — and when it does not, unused capacity may roll over “generally upon the purchase of additional capacity.” Because revenue is recognised “on consumption and not ratably over the term of the contract,” the company says plainly: “we do not have visibility into the timing of revenue recognition from any particular customer”, and “our deferred revenue is not a meaningful indicator of future revenue.” A $9.77 billion backlog does not tell you when — or whether — it becomes revenue.

The strategy is to outrun the efficiency problem by finding more things to run. The platform is “three independently scalable but logically integrated layers across storage, compute, and cloud services,” now carrying Snowpark, Snowflake Intelligence and Cortex Agents, Generation 2 Standard Warehouses, and Snowflake Postgres — opening a “new transactions product category.” It bought Crunchy Data for $164.5 million and Observe, Inc. for approximately $596.2 million. The dependency is stated without hedging: “Our future growth is increasingly dependent on our ability to increase consumption of our platform by building and expanding the AI Data Cloud.”

But one opening it is chasing points back at the meter: “open data formats allow customers to use our platform for compute services without requiring storage.” Interoperability wins deals and unbundles the bill.

The competitors are also the landlords. Snowflake runs on three major public clouds across 53 regional deployments — and competes with “large, well-established, public cloud providers that generally compete in all of our markets, including Amazon Web Services (AWS), Microsoft Azure (Azure), and Google Cloud Platform (GCP),” plus legacy database vendors and new entrants in “markets [that] are highly competitive and rapidly evolving.” Its own named competitive tests: “pricing transparency and optimized price-performance benefits,” “ability to elastically scale up and scale down,” and “interoperability and integrations across open formats.” It has committed publicly to being cheaper per unit of work than last year.

One more place where the bill and the risk sit on different sides of a line. Security is “a core shared responsibility between us and our customers” — Snowflake secures the platform, while “our customers are responsible for selecting, enabling, and configuring security and operational controls for their individual environments.” Where customers use third-party authentication, “we do not have visibility into whether adequate access controls (such as multi-factor authentication (MFA) or network restrictions) are being enforced.” If they configure it wrongly, the consequences land on Snowflake anyway — harm to “our customer relationships, our reputation, and our business, which has occurred in the past and may happen again in the future.”

Every lever available — faster queries, better compression, open formats, transparent pricing — makes the customer happier and the invoice smaller. That is the meeting.
