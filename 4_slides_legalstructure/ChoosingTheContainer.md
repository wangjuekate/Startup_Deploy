# Choosing the Container: What Legal Structure Actually Buys, and What It Costs

Almost everyone starting a venture has heard the sentence *"we should probably set up an LLC."* Almost nobody in that conversation can say what an LLC does for them, which is exactly the condition under which people sign things. Here is the frame to carry instead. **Legal structure is a machine for splitting three things — control, capital, and liability — and you cannot have all three.** Control is who decides — who hires, who fires, who can say no. Capital is who funds it and on what terms. Liability is who holds the bag when it goes wrong. Every entity form and investor term divides those three, and **every choice buys one corner and pays with another.**

Run the instruments through that frame and the pros and cons stop being a memorisation exercise. A **sole proprietorship or general partnership** is free, instant, and the default if you do nothing — total control, unlimited personal liability. That is not theoretical: each partner can bind the partnership, so if one founder signs an office lease, both owe the rent — not half each. An **LLC** buys limited liability with flexible governance and pass-through tax, the workhorse for small ventures, though investors wanting preferred stock and an option pool generally do not want one. A **C-corporation**, usually Delaware, is the form built for outside investment: share classes, a board, an option pool, and in exchange, formality and a steady transfer of control away from you. A **nonprofit corporation** has no owners; its assets are locked to a stated purpose, governed by a board with duties to that purpose. It can raise donations and **cannot sell you equity.** A **public benefit corporation** is a for-profit chartered to pursue a public benefit alongside profit — a constraint on the board, written into the charter.

And then the instruments nobody counts as structure, which do most of the work: the **contract**, the **licence**, the **indemnity**. A licence converts an unbounded legal risk into a priced line item; an indemnity moves a loss onto somebody else's balance sheet. These are structure. They just do not come with a certificate.

The failure mode worth studying is not picking wrong on day one. It is that **structure accretes.** You choose a form for what you are at the beginning — say a nonprofit, because the work is research and the money is donations — and it works, until the thing you are building needs capital at a scale donations cannot reach. So you build a for-profit entity beside it that the nonprofit controls. That entity needs an operating company, which needs a holding company for the raise, which needs a vehicle for one round. Every box was added by competent people solving a real problem, and one day the chart has nine. When *The New York Times* sued over the use of its journalism in December 2023, the complaint named nine defendants at once — a nonprofit, a limited partnership, several limited liability companies, and one of the largest public companies in the world (Grynbaum & Mac, 2023, The New York Times, via [Editor & Publisher](https://www.editorandpublisher.com/stories/the-times-sues-openai-and-microsoft-over-ai-use-of-copyrighted-work,247366)). **You draw the chart in advance. It gets read back in arrears, out loud, by somebody else's lawyer.**

Two asymmetries make this a model rather than a filing cabinet. The first: **liability follows control, not the org chart.** Vicarious liability turns roughly on the *right and ability to control* plus a *direct financial benefit* — so a strategic partner holding a minority stake can be reached because it funded the work, built the infrastructure and sold the output, not because it owned anything. Each decision made it a better partner. Together they made it a defendant. The second: **in a nonprofit or a PBC, your stated purpose is not marketing. It is a charter** — a legal fact about what the entity is for, and a sentence someone may one day read aloud in court in a very different tone of voice.

Which brings us to the pending question. AI has produced ventures whose capital needs look like heavy industry and whose founding promises look like public trusts, and the two do not fit in one container. OpenAI's answer, completed in October 2025, was to restructure so that a nonprofit — the OpenAI Foundation — controls a for-profit public benefit corporation holding the commercial business ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2025/10/28/open-ai-for-profit-microsoft.html), 2025). That is an attempt to buy two corners at once: mission control on top, investable equity underneath. **Whether a board can serve a chartered public purpose while the entity beneath it is raising hundreds of billions is not a question corporate law has answered.** The pressure is not hypothetical — reporting on the terms Meta secured for a Louisiana data centre described tax breaks negotiated with little public input (2026, The New York Times, via [Techmeme](https://www.techmeme.com/260727/p8)): what happens when the entity has scale and the counterparty is a town.

Open to Debate staged the same fight in its Hopkins Forum on breaking up big tech, where Bharat Ramamurti and Matt Stoller argued for structural remedies and Jennifer Huddleston and Geoffrey Manne argued that integration is what makes these products work ([Open to Debate](https://opentodebate.substack.com/p/the-hopkins-forum-tech-titans-or)). Both are arguing about where a boundary belongs. And the clock is short: Anthropic found that **"over a third of survey respondents expect AI to be able to do most or nearly all of their work tasks in the next 12 months"** (Massenkoff et al., 2026, [Anthropic Research](https://www.anthropic.com/research/economic-index-june-2026-report)). Structures chosen this year will be read back under conditions nobody has modelled.

So the question to hold onto, whether you are forming one entity or nine:

**Which corner is this choice buying — and can you say out loud which of the other two you are paying with?**

If you cannot name the payment, you have not understood the deal — and there is always a payment. The founders who get hurt are rarely the ones who picked the wrong form. They are the ones who picked a form for who they were and never re-read it once they became someone else.
