Legal Structure

MENT 3235 · Module 4 · Concept Lecture · Tuesday, September 29

Case: NYT v. Microsoft & OpenAI · Mental model: the Structure Trilemma

The question is not “should I be an LLC.” It is “what am I buying, and what am I paying with.” Same allegation discipline as Module 3 — state it before the case appears.

“We should probably set up an LLC”

  • Hands up if you have heard someone say that sentence. (most hands go up)
  • Keep it up if anyone in that conversation could say what an LLC actually does for you. (the hands go down)
  • Legal structure is the part of entrepreneurship everybody nods along to and nobody can explain — which is exactly the condition under which people sign things.

What to leave with today

Legal structure is not paperwork. It is a machine for splitting up three things — and you cannot have all three.

So every choice is a trade you should be able to name out loud.

Actually run the two hand-raises. The visual of the hands going down is the whole opening; do not narrate it.

The three things — that is the whole subject

Control

Who decides. Who hires, who fires, who picks the direction, who can say no.

Capital

Who funds it, and on what terms they get paid back.

Liability

Who is holding the bag when it goes wrong.

Sole proprietorship: total control, exactly as much capital as you personally have, and unlimited personal liability — your car, your savings, your house.

Corporation: limited liability and access to outside capital — and in exchange you now have shareholders, a board, and duties to people who are not you.

Nothing on that list is free. You do not get limited liability. You buy it, and you pay in control.

Every entity form, investor term, partnership agreement and supplier contract divides these three. Say that, then refuse to add a fourth when someone offers taxes.

The two founders and the handshake

  • Two people start a company. They do not form anything, because forming things costs $500 and they are busy.
  • Legally they have just created a general partnership — a real entity the law will happily recognise on their behalf, whether or not they meant to.
  • Eighteen months later, one of them signs a lease for office space. Not the other one. Just one.

Both of them owe the rent

Each partner can bind the partnership, and each is personally liable for the whole obligation. Not half. The whole thing.

They bought maximum control — nobody could tell them what to do — and paid for it with unlimited, joint, personal liability. And they never knew they were at the till.

Ask what they think happens before you reveal it. “Half the rent” is the usual answer, and being wrong about that is what makes the trilemma stick.

The money that came with a seat

  • A properly formed company raises a big cheque from a big strategic partner — the kind who does not just wire funds but also builds the infrastructure the company runs on.
  • Great deal. The product could not have been built without it.
  • Three years later somebody sues. And the plaintiff's lawyers do not just sue the company. They sue the partner too.

And the argument is not “they own you”

“They had the right and the ability to stop you, and they were making money while you did it.”

That is a real doctrine — vicarious liability. The test is roughly the right and ability to control plus a direct financial benefit.

The partner invested, and built the machine, and helped tune it, and put the output in their own products. Every one of those made them a better partner. All four together made them a defendant.

Let the quoted sentence sit for a beat before you name the doctrine. Then point out what it attaches to: control, never the org chart.

The instruments, honestly

Sole proprietorship / general partnership

Free, instant, and the default if you do nothing. Full control, unlimited personal liability.

LLC

Limited liability, flexible governance, pass-through tax. The workhorse — but investors wanting preferred stock and option pools usually do not want one.

C-corporation

The form built for outside investment: shares, classes, boards, option pools. Costs you formality and, over time, control.

Nonprofit corporation

No owners. Assets locked to a stated purpose, a board with duties to that purpose. It can raise donations. It cannot sell you equity.

Public benefit corporation

A for-profit chartered to pursue a stated public benefit alongside profit. A hybrid — and a deliberate constraint on the board.

The ones nobody counts

Contract · licence · indemnity · warranty. A licence converts an unbounded legal risk into a priced line item. An indemnity moves a loss onto someone else's balance sheet. These are structure — they just do not come with a certificate.

The last card does most of the work in real ventures and gets none of the attention. Spend your time there, not on the entity list.

Structure is not built. It accretes.

  • You pick a structure for what you are on day one. A nonprofit — the work is research, the money is donations, the mission is the point. And it works.
  • Then the thing you are building needs capital at a scale donations cannot reach.
  • A nonprofit has no owners and cannot sell equity — so you build something next to it. Then that needs an operating company. Then a holding company for the raise. Then a shell for one round.
  • You look up one day and there are nine of them.

None of it is illegal, and none of it is stupid

Every box was added for a real reason by people solving a real problem. That is precisely why it is worth studying.

Nobody ever sits down, reads the whole chart back, and asks the only question that matters: who is actually in control here — and does the chart agree?

The nine entities are not a scandal; they are an accretion. That framing is what stops Thursday turning into a morality contest.

The mental model — the Structure Trilemma

Put any structural choice on the triangle and ask which corner it buys, and which corner pays.

Beat 1: the three corners only. Beat 2: pick two — make them argue that you cannot have all three. Beat 4 is the case in one line.

Which corner does it buy, and which one pays?

Stay a sole proprietor

Buys control. Pays with liability protection.

Incorporate

Buys liability protection. Pays with formality, and some control.

Raise a priced round

Buys capital. Pays with control.

Take strategic money from your infrastructure provider

Buys capital and capability. Pays with control — and, per the doctrine, exposure.

Licence an input instead of taking it

Buys liability protection. Pays with capital — you pay the fee.

Push the risk into a subsidiary

Buys some liability protection — only against money judgments. An injunction does not care about your boxes.

Do these as a call-and-response. Give them the choice, make the room supply the payment. The last card is the one they get wrong.

The asymmetries that make it a model

Liability follows control, not the org chart

You can draw any boundary you like. If you kept the right and the ability to say no, and you were being paid, the boundary is a drawing.

Which is how a strategic partner becomes a defendant while holding a minority stake — the theory does not run through ownership at all.

Chosen in advance, tested in arrears

You pick it when you are small, optimistic, and solving today's problem. It is read back to you when you are large, adversarial, and being asked about a decision made by someone who has left.

In a nonprofit or a PBC, your stated purpose is not marketing. It is a charter. The mission statement is a legal fact about what the entity is for — write it like one.

Every “we will always” in a founding document is a sentence someone may read aloud in a courtroom in a very different tone of voice. That is Thursday's opening exhibit.

The one question the model makes you ask

Not “what entity should we be?” — but which corner is this choice buying, and can I say out loud which of the other two I am paying with?

If you cannot name the payment, you have not understood the deal. There is always a payment.

Apply it to one decision each team has already made — usually a co-founder split or a client contract. They will find a payment nobody named.

Next: the simulation

  • A real complaint, in which a 172-year-old newspaper sues nine defendants at once — a Delaware nonprofit, a limited partnership, six LLCs, and one of the largest public companies in the world — over a product built out of text.
  • You will see a founding promise from 2015 quoted back at a defendant in 2023. A partner reached through control rather than ownership. And a remedy that no entity boundary stops.

How we talk about this case

These are accusations in a filed complaint. Nothing has been proved and no court has ruled.

Every sentence about the facts begins “the complaint alleges.”

Your job is not to decide who wins. It is to decide how a company should be structured — and to be precise about which corner you are buying, and which one you are spending.
