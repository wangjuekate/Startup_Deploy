Case: The New York Times Company v. Microsoft Corporation, OpenAI, Inc. et al., No. 1:23-cv-11195 (S.D.N.Y.), complaint filed December 27, 2023

⚠️ Everything on this page is an allegation from a filed complaint. Nothing has been proved and no court has ruled. Say “the complaint alleges” every time you state a fact. That discipline is graded.

You own one decision: how much control the company takes over a partner’s system. Everyone on your team has a different page. Yours is control. Read it, pick one option, and be ready to defend it with something from the document.

What the complaint alleges

Start with the strange fact

Microsoft did not train these models. The complaint says so in its own words — the two companies had a division of labour, and the modelling was on the other side of it. Yet Microsoft is defendant number one in the caption.

Your page is about why.

What the partnership actually was

“Microsoft is the sole cloud computing provider for OpenAI. Microsoft and OpenAI collaborated to design the supercomputing systems powered by Microsoft’s cloud computer platform Azure, which were used to train all OpenAI’s GPT models after GPT-1.”

— ¶ 68

Satya Nadella, quoted twice:

“We built the infrastructure to train their models. They’re innovating on the algorithms and the training of these frontier models.”

“They do the foundation models, and we [Microsoft] do a lot of work around them, including the tooling around responsible AI and AI safety.”

— ¶¶ 68, 71

And the machine itself:

Microsoft built this supercomputer “in collaboration with and exclusively for OpenAI,” and “designed [it] specifically to train that company’s AI models.” — “a single system with more than 285,000 CPU cores, 10,000 GPUs and 400 gigabits per second of network connectivity for each GPU server.”

— ¶ 70

“To ensure that the supercomputing system suited OpenAI’s needs, Microsoft needed to test the system, both independently and in collaboration with OpenAI software engineers.”

— ¶ 71

Every one of those facts is a description of a good partner. Bespoke, exclusive, deeply integrated, jointly tested. Now watch what the complaint builds out of them.

The allegation that should decide your page

“To the extent that Microsoft did not select the works used to train the GPT models, it acted in self-described ‘partnership’ with OpenAI respecting that selection, knew or was willfully blind to the identity of the selected works… and/or had the right and ability to prevent OpenAI from using any particular work for training by virtue of its physical control of the supercomputer it developed for that purpose and its legal and financial influence over the OpenAI Defendants.”

— ¶ 94

And then, in the count itself:

“Microsoft controlled, directed, and profited from the infringement perpetrated by the OpenAI Defendants. Microsoft controls and directs the supercomputing platform used to store, process, and reproduce the training datasets… Microsoft profited from the infringement… by incorporating the infringing GPT models trained on Times Works into its own product offerings, including Bing Chat.”

— ¶ 170 (Count II — Vicarious Copyright Infringement)

That is the doctrine, in two ingredients: the right and ability to control, plus a direct financial benefit. Notice what is not an ingredient. Ownership. The complaint elsewhere pleads that Microsoft holds only a minority stake in OpenAI Global LLC. The theory does not go through the cap table at all.

The uncomfortable middle

So consider the three positions you could occupy as the technical partner:

Position Control Exposure under this theory
Neutral utility — general-purpose, no bespoke build none claimed weakest
Deep partner who exercises control — reviews inputs, can and does say no real and used you carry it, but you can prevent the harm
Deep partner who has control and never uses it real and unused “right and ability to prevent” — all of the exposure, none of the protection

Read ¶ 94 one more time and ask which of those three rows it describes.

Your question

Liability attaches to control, not to the org chart. How much control do you take over a partner’s system?

Your four choices — pick one

  • (A) Stay a neutral utility: general-purpose infrastructure, no bespoke build, no involvement in what the customer runs on it.
  • (B) Push the whole obligation into the contract — warranties on the training data, audit rights, and indemnities that follow whoever chose the inputs.
  • (C) Take as much operational control as the partnership will give us and rely on the separate corporate entities to keep the liability where it belongs.
  • (D) Decide deliberately: either hold the control and exercise it — with real input review and the ability to say no — or contract it away and stay a utility. The pleaded theory is “right and ability to prevent”, so unexercised control is the worst of both.

Before you answer

A competent lead does four things, in this order:

  1. Name the corner your choice buys — control, capital, or liability protection.
  2. Name the corner it pays with. There is always a payment.
  3. Cite an allegation from this page. Not a feeling — something from the document.
  4. State one thing that would change your answer. If nothing would, you are not reasoning.

One test before you commit. There are nine defendants on this caption, and the entities were drawn by good lawyers. Ask yourself the flat question: what did the boundaries accomplish? If your answer to your own decision relies on a boundary doing work, look at ¶ 94 and check whether that is a thing boundaries do.
