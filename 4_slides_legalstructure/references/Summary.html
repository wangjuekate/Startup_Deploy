Suing the Chart

On December 27, 2023, The New York Times filed a complaint naming nine defendants. Not one company — nine: a Delaware nonprofit corporation, a limited partnership, six limited liability companies, and one of the largest public companies in the world.

That is the case. Everything else follows from the shape of the chart.

The Times is a newspaper with a business model older than any defendant on the page. Its journalism “is the work of thousands of journalists, whose employment costs hundreds of millions of dollars per year.” For decades it has been paid for that work through a mechanism so ordinary it barely gets noticed: “The Times has licensed its content under negotiated licensing agreements… Third parties, including large tech platforms, pay The Times significant royalties… in exchange for the right to use Times content for narrowly defined purposes.” The prices are not mysterious. Through the Copyright Clearance Center, a photocopy licence runs “about ten dollars per article”; one article on a commercial website for a year, “several thousand dollars.” The Times lets search engines index it. But it “has never given permission to any entity, including Defendants, to use its content for GenAI purposes.” In April 2023 it approached Microsoft and OpenAI “to explore… commercial terms and technological guardrails.” “These efforts have not produced a resolution.”

Eight months later it sued: “Defendants’ unlawful use of The Times’s work to create artificial intelligence products that compete with it threatens The Times’s ability to provide that service.”

Now look at what it was suing. “The OpenAI Defendants consist of a web of interrelated Delaware entities.” OpenAI, Inc. — the Delaware nonprofit formed in December 2015 — “indirectly owns and controls all other OpenAI entities.” Beneath it sit OpenAI LP (2019), “operated for profit”; OpenAI GP, LLC, which “manages and operates the day-to-day business”; OpenAI, LLC (2020), which “owns, sells, licenses, and monetizes… ChatGPT” and the API; OpenAI OpCo LLC; OpenAI Global LLC (2022), in which “Microsoft has a minority stake”; OAI Corporation, LLC; and OpenAI Holdings, LLC, whose members include Aestas, LLC — “a Delaware shell company formed for the purpose of executing a $495 million capital raise.” Microsoft, the ninth defendant, “has invested at least $13 billion in OpenAI.”

The chart is a machine for splitting three things apart: who controls, who funds, who is liable.

How it got that way is the second act. In December 2015 OpenAI was formed as a “non-profit artificial intelligence research company” with “$1 billion in seed money” from founders including Elon Musk, Reid Hoffman, Sam Altman and Greg Brockman. The founding announcement is quoted because it is now evidence: “Our goal is to advance digital intelligence in the way that is most likely to benefit humanity as a whole, unconstrained by a need to generate financial return.” Researchers “will be strongly encouraged to publish their work”; “patents (if any) will be shared with the world.”

Then, the complaint pleads, “just three years after its founding, OpenAI shed its exclusively nonprofit status. It created OpenAI LP… a for-profit company dedicated to conducting the lion’s share of OpenAI’s operations… and to raising capital from investors seeking a return.” What followed was “an intricate web of for-profit holding, operating, and shell companies… and grant[s] OpenAI’s investors (most prominently, Microsoft) authority and influence over OpenAI’s operations” — “a commercial enterprise valued as high as $90 billion, with revenues projected to be over $1 billion in 2024.” Openness ended on the same schedule: GPT-1 and GPT-2 were open-sourced in 2018 and 2019, and “OpenAI changed course in 2020, starting with the release of GPT-3 shortly after OpenAI LP and other for-profit OpenAI entities were formed and took control of product design and development.”

Which brings the sting into view, aimed squarely at the partner who wrote no code.

Microsoft trained nothing. It is “the sole cloud computing provider for OpenAI,” and built the supercomputer “in collaboration with and exclusively for OpenAI”, “designed [it] specifically to train that company’s AI models” — “more than 285,000 CPU cores, 10,000 GPUs.” Satya Nadella: “We built the infrastructure to train their models. They’re innovating on the algorithms.” Every one of those facts describes a serious technical partnership. Paragraph 94 turns them into a theory of liability: Microsoft “had the right and ability to prevent OpenAI from using any particular work for training by virtue of its physical control of the supercomputer it developed for that purpose and its legal and financial influence over the OpenAI Defendants.” Count II, vicarious infringement: “Microsoft controlled, directed, and profited from the infringement.”

Control plus benefit. The facts that made Microsoft a real partner made it a defendant — and no entity boundary on the chart sits between them.

The seven counts spread across that chart deliberately: direct copyright infringement (§ 501), vicarious infringement, contributory infringement twice over, DMCA removal of copyright-management information (17 U.S.C. § 1202), common-law unfair competition by misappropriation, and trademark dilution (15 U.S.C. § 1125(c)). The models “can generate output that recites Times content verbatim… and mimics its expressive style.” And when they get it wrong, that is a trademark injury too: “misattributing content to The Times that it did not, in fact, publish… In plain English, it’s misinformation” — pleaded as dilution by tarnishment over the marks “The New York Times” (Reg. No. 5,912,366) and “nytimes.com.”

And the remedy is not a bill. Alongside statutory and compensatory damages, restitution, disgorgement and a permanent injunction, The Times asks for “destruction under 17 U.S.C. § 503(b) of all GPT or other LLM models and training sets that incorporate Times Works.”

Ten dollars an article was on the table in April 2023. This was on the table in December.
