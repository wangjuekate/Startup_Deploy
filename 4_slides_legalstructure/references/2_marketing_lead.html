Case: The New York Times Company v. Microsoft Corporation, OpenAI, Inc. et al., No. 1:23-cv-11195 (S.D.N.Y.), complaint filed December 27, 2023

⚠️ Everything on this page is an allegation from a filed complaint. Nothing has been proved and no court has ruled. Say “the complaint alleges” every time you state a fact. That discipline is graded.

You own one decision: what the company says it is for. Everyone on your team has a different page. Yours is purpose. Read it, pick one option, and be ready to defend it with something from the document.

What the complaint alleges

The sentence you wrote in 2015

The complaint quotes the founding announcement of December 11, 2015 — a press release — word for word:

“Our goal is to advance digital intelligence in the way that is most likely to benefit humanity as a whole, unconstrained by a need to generate financial return. Since our research is free from financial obligations, we can better focus on a positive human impact.”

— ¶ 56

Along with two specific promises:

Researchers “will be strongly encouraged to publish their work, whether as papers, blog posts, or code” and “patents (if any) will be shared with the world.”

— ¶ 56

Read those as a marketer for a second. They are excellent copy. Clear, generous, memorable, differentiating. Every line does its job.

And here is the sentence that follows it

“Despite its early promises of altruism, OpenAI quickly became a multi-billion-dollar for-profit business built in large part on the unlicensed exploitation of copyrighted works belonging to The Times and others.”

— ¶ 57

That is not a paraphrase of your position. That is your own press release, used as the setup for the accusation. The 2015 language is doing work in this document that you did not commission.

The change the complaint says came with the structure

“With the transition to for-profit status came another change: OpenAI also ended its commitment to openness. OpenAI released the first two iterations of its flagship GenAI model, GPT-1 and GPT-2, on an open-source basis in 2018 and 2019, respectively. But OpenAI changed course in 2020, starting with the release of GPT-3 shortly after OpenAI LP and other for-profit OpenAI entities were formed and took control of product design and development.”

— ¶ 58

“GPT-3.5 and GPT-4… yet Defendants have kept their design and training entirely a secret.”

— ¶ 59

The complaint is not merely observing that the story changed. It is pleading a cause: the entities changed, and the promise changed with them.

Why this is a legal page and not a communications page

The entity being quoted is a Delaware nonprofit corporation. A nonprofit has no owners; its assets are dedicated to a stated purpose, and its board owes duties to that purpose. The same is true, in a different way, of a public benefit corporation — a for-profit that is chartered to pursue a named public benefit.

Which means: for these forms, the mission statement is not positioning. It is closer to a charter document. You do not get to treat it as copy that gets refreshed when the strategy does.

And the other place your brand shows up in this complaint

You should know that trademark is in here too — on the receiving end. The Times pleads dilution by tarnishment (Count VII) over registered marks “The New York Times” (Reg. No. 5,912,366), “nytimes” and “nytimes.com”, because the products allegedly misattribute content:

“…causing The Times commercial and competitive injury by misattributing content to The Times that it did not, in fact, publish. In AI parlance, this is called a ‘hallucination.’ In plain English, it’s misinformation.”

— ¶ 136

A name is an asset somebody else can damage on your behalf. Hold that thought — it is why the other side of this case cares about attribution as much as about copying.

Your question

Your company’s structure is about to change. When a stated purpose is a legal fact, what do you say?

Your four choices — pick one

  • (A) Keep the founding mission language exactly as it is. It is what the brand was built on, and changing it would concede that something changed.
  • (B) Publish the governance arrangements themselves — who controls what, and what the mission constrains — so the promise and the chart cannot drift apart.
  • (C) Say less about purpose altogether and let the products speak, since any mission language can be read back as a commitment.
  • (D) Say only what the structure can actually deliver, and restate it the day the structure changes — because a stated purpose is a charter document, and this complaint quotes the 2015 promise back at a 2023 defendant.

Before you answer

A competent lead does four things, in this order:

  1. Name the corner your choice buys — control, capital, or liability protection.
  2. Name the corner it pays with. There is always a payment.
  3. Cite an allegation from this page. Not a feeling — something from the document.
  4. State one thing that would change your answer. If nothing would, you are not reasoning.

One test before you commit. Take the sentence you would put out and imagine it in eleven-point type, indented, inside a paragraph numbered ¶ 56 of somebody’s complaint. If it still says what you meant, publish it. If it only works in the voice it was written in, it is not a purpose — it is a slogan, and slogans do not survive being read aloud by opposing counsel.
