Financial Lead

Detailed reference, not required in full during the timed activity. Start with the short student packet; use this page to investigate or cite supporting evidence.

Case: The New York Times Company v. Microsoft Corporation, OpenAI, Inc. et al., No. 1:23-cv-11195 (S.D.N.Y.), complaint filed December 27, 2023

⚠️ Everything on this page is an allegation from a filed complaint. Nothing has been proved and no court has ruled. Say “the complaint alleges” every time you state a fact. That discipline is graded.

You own one decision: where the next dollar of capital comes from. Everyone on your team has a different page. Yours is the money. Read it, pick one option, and be ready to defend it with something from the document.

What the complaint alleges

Where the money started

“OpenAI was formed in December 2015 as a ‘non-profit artificial intelligence research company.’ OpenAI started with $1 billion in seed money from its founders, a group of some of the wealthiest technology entrepreneurs and investors and companies like Amazon Web Services and InfoSys.”

— ¶ 55

A nonprofit corporation has no owners. It cannot sell you equity. It can take donations and grants, and its assets are locked to a stated purpose. That is a real constraint on where the next dollar can come from, and it is the constraint that the rest of this page is about.

What happened when donations stopped being enough

“Just three years after its founding, OpenAI shed its exclusively nonprofit status. It created OpenAI LP in March 2019, a for-profit company dedicated to conducting the lion’s share of OpenAI’s operations—including product development—and to raising capital from investors seeking a return.”

— ¶ 57

And then the sentence you should read twice:

“OpenAI’s corporate structure grew into an intricate web of for-profit holding, operating, and shell companies that manage OpenAI’s day-to-day operations and grant OpenAI’s investors (most prominently, Microsoft) authority and influence over OpenAI’s operations, all while raising billions in capital from investors.”

— ¶ 57

Read what that sentence does. It puts capital and authority and influence in the same clause, as two halves of one transaction. That is not the plaintiff being unfair. That is what the deal was.

The chart the money built

The complaint pleads nine defendants — “a web of interrelated Delaware entities” (¶ 17):

Entity Form Pleaded role
OpenAI, Inc. Delaware nonprofit “indirectly owns and controls all other OpenAI entities”
OpenAI LP limited partnership (2019) “operated for profit and is controlled by OpenAI Inc.”
OpenAI GP, LLC LLC general partner; “manages and operates the day-to-day business”
OpenAI, LLC LLC (Sept 2020) “owns, sells, licenses, and monetizes… ChatGPT”
OpenAI OpCo LLC LLC wholly owned by OpenAI Inc.; directs OpenAI, LLC
OpenAI Global LLC LLC (Dec 2022) “Microsoft Corporation has a minority stake”
OAI Corporation, LLC LLC sole member is OpenAI Holdings, LLC
OpenAI Holdings, LLC LLC members: OpenAI, Inc. and Aestas, LLC
Aestas Management Co. “a Delaware shell company formed for the purpose of executing a $495 million capital raise”

Note the last row. One of those boxes exists for one round of funding. That is what “structure accretes” looks like on paper.

The strategic dollar, and what it came with

“Microsoft has invested at least $13 billion in OpenAI.”

— ¶ 15

And what that relationship is, as pleaded: “Microsoft is the sole cloud computing provider for OpenAI” (¶ 68); it built the training supercomputer “in collaboration with and exclusively for OpenAI” (¶ 70).

The result, per the complaint: “a commercial enterprise valued as high as $90 billion, with revenues projected to be over $1 billion in 2024.”

By any ordinary measure, the capital strategy worked.

And here is the bill

“Microsoft… had the right and ability to prevent OpenAI from using any particular work for training by virtue of its physical control of the supercomputer it developed for that purpose and its legal and financial influence over the OpenAI Defendants.”

— ¶ 94

Look at what is doing the work in that sentence. Not the stake — the complaint says Microsoft’s stake in OpenAI Global LLC is a minority one. “Legal and financial influence.” The money is being used as evidence of control, and the control is being used as a basis for liability.

Your question

Capital never arrives alone. Where does the next dollar come from — and what does it take?

Your four choices — pick one

  • (A) Keep the charitable parent nominally in control on the org chart and let the for-profit subsidiaries take whatever terms investors require.
  • (B) Raise against a defined scope, and treat the governance rights the investor wants as part of the price — because the complaint reaches Microsoft through “legal and financial influence”, not through its stake.
  • (C) Take the strategic money and the compute together — it is the cheapest capital available and the alternative is not building the thing at all.
  • (D) Diversify the capital base so no single provider holds both the money and the infrastructure we cannot operate without.

Before you answer

A competent lead does four things, in this order:

  1. Name the corner your choice buys — control, capital, or liability protection.
  2. Name the corner it pays with. There is always a payment.
  3. Cite an allegation from this page. Not a feeling — something from the document.
  4. State one thing that would change your answer. If nothing would, you are not reasoning.

One test before you commit. Take your chosen option and ask: if a plaintiff’s lawyer read this arrangement back to a judge in three years, what sentence would they build out of it? Somebody wrote ¶ 94 by reading a funding relationship. Read yours the same way before you sign it.
