Operation Lead

Detailed reference, not required in full during the timed activity. Start with the short student packet; use this page to investigate or cite supporting evidence.

Case: The New York Times Company v. Microsoft Corporation, OpenAI, Inc. et al., No. 1:23-cv-11195 (S.D.N.Y.), complaint filed December 27, 2023

⚠️ Everything on this page is an allegation from a filed complaint. Nothing has been proved and no court has ruled. Say “the complaint alleges” every time you state a fact. That discipline is graded.

You own one decision: how the company obtains rights to the things it is built out of. Everyone on your team has a different page. Yours is the inputs. Read it, pick one option, and be ready to defend it with something from the document.

What the complaint alleges

There was a market, and it had prices

This is the part everyone skips. Before you decide whether to licence something, find out whether you could have. Here, the complaint pleads that you could:

“The Times requires third parties to obtain permission before using Times content and trademarks for commercial purposes, and for decades The Times has licensed its content under negotiated licensing agreements. These agreements help ensure that The Times controls how, where, and for how long its content and brand appears and that it receives fair compensation… Third parties, including large tech platforms, pay The Times significant royalties under these agreements in exchange for the right to use Times content for narrowly defined purposes. The agreements prohibit uses beyond those authorized purposes.”

— ¶ 50

And there is a published, off-the-shelf rate card:

Through the Copyright Clearance Center, “a for-profit business can acquire a CCC license to make a photocopy of Times content for internal or external distribution in exchange for a licensing fee of about ten dollars per article. A CCC license to post a single Times article on a commercial website for up to a year costs several thousand dollars.”

— ¶ 51

Ten dollars an article. Write that number down. You are going to need it when somebody on your team argues that licensing was impractical.

What was permitted, and what was not

“While The Times, like virtually all online publishers, permits search engines to access its content for the limited purpose of surfacing it in traditional search results, The Times has never given permission to any entity, including Defendants, to use its content for GenAI purposes.”

— ¶ 53

That distinction is the whole operational question. Access is not a licence. “You could reach it” and “you were permitted to use it for this” are different facts, and only the second one is a contract.

Note also what is not in this complaint: there is no breach-of-contract count. Seven counts, and not one of them is breach. That is not an oversight — it is because, as pleaded, there was never an agreement to breach.

And they asked

“The Times reached out to Microsoft and OpenAI in April 2023 to raise intellectual property concerns and explore the possibility of an amicable resolution, with commercial terms and technological guardrails that would allow a mutually beneficial value exchange between Defendants and The Times. These efforts have not produced a resolution.”

— ¶ 54

A counterparty who has a rate card, a decades-old licensing practice, and who initiated the conversation is about as available as a counterparty gets.

What the complaint says was saved by not doing it

“Each Defendant has reaped substantial savings by taking and using—at no cost—New York Times content to create their LLMs. Times journalism is the work of thousands of journalists, whose employment costs hundreds of millions of dollars per year.… Defendants have effectively avoided spending the billions of dollars that The Times invested in creating that work.”

— ¶ 144

The savings are real. That is exactly why this is a decision and not a lecture. Somebody chose the free input over the priced one, and the choice was defensible on the day it was made.

And what the bill looks like

The prayer for relief asks for statutory and compensatory damages, restitution and disgorgement — and:

“Ordering destruction under 17 U.S.C. § 503(b) of all GPT or other LLM models and training sets that incorporate Times Works.”

A licence has a number on it. This does not.

Your question

A licence converts an unbounded exposure into a priced line item. How do you obtain your inputs?

Your four choices — pick one

  • (A) Build the provenance system first — know exactly what went in and under what terms — so we can licence, exclude or replace any input on demand.
  • (B) Take the public web now and negotiate later from a position of strength — the product exists either way, and a settlement after launch is cheaper than a licence before it.
  • (C) Licence the inputs before the build, on defined-purpose terms — the market rate was published and the counterparty asked to negotiate in April 2023; a priced contract term is the only version of this exposure that has a ceiling.
  • (D) Rely on the doctrinal argument that training is transformative, and reserve against the risk that a court disagrees.

Before you answer

A competent lead does four things, in this order:

  1. Name the corner your choice buys — control, capital, or liability protection.
  2. Name the corner it pays with. There is always a payment.
  3. Cite an allegation from this page. Not a feeling — something from the document.
  4. State one thing that would change your answer. If nothing would, you are not reasoning.

One test before you commit. Put a number on your choice. A licence has one — ¶ 51 gives you the order of magnitude. Now put a number on the alternative, and notice that you cannot, because the remedy sought is not a payment at all. The corner you are really buying here is not capital. It is a ceiling.
