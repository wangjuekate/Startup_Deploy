Detailed reference, not required in full during the timed activity. Start with the short student packet; use this page to investigate or cite supporting evidence.

Case: The New York Times Company v. Microsoft Corporation, OpenAI, Inc. et al., No. 1:23-cv-11195 (S.D.N.Y.), complaint filed December 27, 2023

⚠️ Everything on this page is an allegation from a filed complaint. Nothing has been proved and no court has ruled. Say “the complaint alleges” every time you state a fact — and hold your four leads to it too. That discipline is graded, and you are the one who enforces it in the room.

You answer no functional question of your own. The other four leads each own one decision and one page. You own the whole case — and the order in which its problems get solved. Your page is the overview. Read it before they present.

What kind of document this is

Not a filing. Not a ruling. A complaint — the document one side files to start a lawsuit, written by the plaintiff’s lawyers to persuade. Everything in it is an accusation.

COMPLAINT · JURY TRIAL DEMANDED. Filed December 27, 2023, U.S. District Court for the Southern District of New York. 69 pages.

“Defendants’ unlawful use of The Times’s work to create artificial intelligence products that compete with it threatens The Times’s ability to provide that service.”

— ¶ 2

Note the caption. Not one defendant. Nine. A publisher suing an org chart is the module — and every count picks out a different subset of it.

The nine defendants, as pleaded (¶¶ 15–25)

“The OpenAI Defendants consist of a web of interrelated Delaware entities.” — ¶ 17

Entity Form What the complaint alleges
Microsoft Corporation public corporation “has invested at least $13 billion”; “the sole cloud computing provider for OpenAI”
OpenAI, Inc. Delaware nonprofit (Dec 2015) “indirectly owns and controls all other OpenAI entities”
OpenAI LP limited partnership (2019) “operated for profit and is controlled by OpenAI Inc.”
OpenAI GP, LLC LLC general partner; “manages and operates the day-to-day business”; “OpenAI, Inc. uses OpenAI GP LLC to control OpenAI LP”
OpenAI, LLC LLC (Sept 2020) “owns, sells, licenses, and monetizes… ChatGPT, ChatGPT Enterprise, and… API tools”

OpenAI Holdings, LLC LLC members: OpenAI, Inc. and Aestas, LLC — “a Delaware shell company formed for the purpose of executing a $495 million capital raise”

Read the chart as a machine for splitting three things: who controls, who funds, who is liable.

The whole case on one page

How the structure got that way (¶¶ 55–58)

  • Dec 2015 — founded as a “non-profit artificial intelligence research company” with “$1 billion in seed money.”
  • The promise, quoted: “unconstrained by a need to generate financial return”; researchers “strongly encouraged to publish”; “patents (if any) will be shared with the world.”
  • March 2019 — “shed its exclusively nonprofit status”; created OpenAI LP “to rais[e] capital from investors seeking a return.”
  • What it bought and cost: an “intricate web of for-profit holding, operating, and shell companies” that “grant OpenAI’s investors (most prominently, Microsoft) authority and influence over OpenAI’s operations, all while raising billions in capital.”
  • Result pleaded: valued “as high as $90 billion”, revenue projected “over $1 billion in 2024.” And “with the transition to for-profit status came another change: OpenAI also ended its commitment to openness” — open-source through GPT-2, closed from GPT-3.

The seven counts

# Count Against
I Direct copyright infringement (17 U.S.C. § 501) all
II Vicarious infringement Microsoft + six OpenAI entities
III Contributory infringement Microsoft
IV Contributory infringement OpenAI defendants
V DMCA § 1202 — removal of copyright-management information all
VI Common-law unfair competition by misappropriation all
VII Trademark dilution (15 U.S.C. § 1125(c)) all

There is no breach-of-contract count. As pleaded, there was never a contract — “The Times has never given permission to any entity, including Defendants, to use its content for GenAI purposes.” (¶ 53)

The licence that existed (¶¶ 50–54) — “for decades The Times has licensed its content under negotiated licensing agreements” for “narrowly defined purposes”; CCC rates from “about ten dollars per article” to “several thousand dollars” for a year on a commercial site; The Times “reached out to Microsoft and OpenAI in April 2023” seeking “commercial terms and technological guardrails” — “These efforts have not produced a resolution.”

How the complaint reaches Microsoft, which trained nothing (¶¶ 68–71, 94, 170) — sole cloud provider; supercomputer built “in collaboration with and exclusively for OpenAI” (285,000 CPU cores, 10,000 GPUs); Nadella: “We built the infrastructure to train their models.” Then:

“…had the right and ability to prevent OpenAI from using any particular work for training by virtue of its physical control of the supercomputer… and its legal and financial influence.” — ¶ 94

“Microsoft controlled, directed, and profited from the infringement.” — ¶ 170

Not ownership. Control plus benefit.

What is alleged downstream — outputs that “recite Times content verbatim”, summarise it and “mimic its expressive style” (Exhibit J); “misattributing content to The Times that it did not, in fact, publish… In plain English, it’s misinformation” (¶ 136); copyright-management information “removed” in building the datasets; and savings from “taking and using—at no cost” work that costs “hundreds of millions of dollars per year” to produce (¶ 144).

What The Times asks for — statutory and compensatory damages, restitution, disgorgement, a permanent injunction, and:

“Ordering destruction under 17 U.S.C. § 503(b) of all GPT or other LLM models and training sets that incorporate Times Works.”

That is a remedy aimed at the asset. No box on the chart above sits between it and the model.

Your task, in three parts

Part 1 — Rank them 1 to 4

Put the four problems in the order they must be resolved for a company whose product is built on inputs it does not own, from the one that has to be fixed first down to the one that can wait longest. Each problem is used exactly once. Then give one sentence justifying rank #1 — and that sentence must cite something specific from the complaint.

Part 3 — Name the corner

Out loud, to the team: which corner of the trilemma — control, capital, or liability protection — did this structure actually buy? Which of the other two did it spend to get it? And what would have to have been true for that to have been the right trade?

How to run the room

  • Rank by where the exposure is created, not by where it lands.
  • Enforce the word “alleges.” A lead who states an allegation as fact gets corrected in the room. You are graded on this too.
  • Watch for the entity answer. Somebody will propose a subsidiary, a holding company, or a restructure. Ask them the flat question: there are nine entities in this caption. What did the boundaries accomplish?
  • Watch for “we’d settle.” Ask what number they are settling against. Item 3 of the prayer for relief is not a number — it asks the court to order the models destroyed.
  • Watch for “the licence was impractical.” ¶ 51 puts a CCC licence at about ten dollars an article, and ¶ 54 says the counterparty asked to negotiate in April 2023.
  • Watch for “Microsoft is just the cloud provider.” ¶ 94 is built entirely out of facts that make Microsoft a better partner. Make the room sit with that.
  • You are allowed to disagree with your own team. The ranking is yours to sign.
