The idea was never the hard part

An idea is a free option

Every semester somebody comes to office hours with an idea they will not describe until I promise not to steal it. I always promise, and I have never once been tempted.

Selling GLP-1 medications direct to consumers online was not a secret. Hundreds of people had that idea at the same time.

Execution is where you pay

Medvi did $401M in sales in 2025 with two employees — the founder and his brother (Griffith, 2026, The New York Times).

What separated them was not insight. It was pharmacy relationships that functioned, ads that converted, payments that cleared, and a support queue answered on a Sunday.

And the second half of that story matters too: within days, other outlets were raising allegations about the company's practices. A story about execution travels faster than execution does. (Pre-class reading: The Idea Was Never the Hard Part)

Ask who read the blog. Ask who believes the $401M number means what it appears to mean. Revenue measures what customers paid, not what they received.

What got cheap, and what did not

In a survey of roughly 81,000 people, the most common gain was not speed but scope — work people previously could not do at all (Massenkoff & Huang, 2026, Anthropic Research).

Beat 1 is the setup. Beat 2 should feel like good news and then land as bad news: the barrier that slowed you down was also protecting you. Beat 3 is the course. Beat 4 is the syllabus.

So the advantage moved — and this is where it moved to

  • If everyone in this section can now build the thing, the barrier that used to protect your idea is gone — and it was protecting you from competitors as much as it was slowing you down.
  • What is left is judgment under incomplete information: which step of the value chain to own, which cost to out-operate, which secret to keep, what to charge for, how much of next year to promise away.
  • No tool makes those calls for you, because each one is a trade with no correct answer — only a defensible one.

What this course is not

It is not a semester of writing a plan nobody will read.

It is fifteen weeks of making the eight decisions a venture actually has to make, on real numbers, out loud, in front of people who will push back.

The objection to head off: “I am not going to found a company.” The answer is that these eight decisions are made inside every firm, by whoever is trusted to make them.

Eight modules, eight mental models

A mental model is not a framework to fill in. It is a named tension you can carry into a room where nobody agrees.

Beat 2 is the midterm's four topics, beat 3 the final's four. Say plainly that the team project is graded against exactly these sections.

How to ace this course

60% is a loop you repeat every week. 40% is three team milestones. There is no exam to save you at the end.

Eight beats: walk the loop 1-3, total it at 4, then the milestones 5-6, the total at 7, and only then the make-up path at 8. Do not show beat 8 early or it becomes the takeaway.

The 60%: what "prepared" means

Blog comments — 10%

Before class: 50–100 words with a clear opinion and evidence behind it. "Interesting post" is not a comment.

In-class participation — 20%

Be a decision-maker in the simulation, not a spectator. Use evidence when you defend a recommendation.

Simulation reflections — 30%

After class: what you did, what it cost, what you would do differently next time. Specific beats polished.

Read one strong and one weak blog comment aloud in Week 2. Setting the bar by example saves a semester of arguing about grades.

The 40%: your team's venture

  • Teams of 4–5, developing one new venture concept all semester.
  • Midterm pitch video + report — 20%, Week 7.
  • Final pitch video + report — 20%, Week 15 and finals week.

The through-line

Each module hands your team a tool. The midterm is the first honest test of whether the idea survives contact with those tools; the final is whether you fixed what broke.

Team formation happens Thursday of Week 1 — point them at the TeamFormationResources deck.

What you should be able to do by December

  • Develop venture plans that address market, operational, legal, financial and technology decisions.
  • Analyze markets and value chains — customer needs, research, and where a venture actually sits in the chain.
  • Design business models that connect operations, revenue and customer value.
  • Navigate legal and IP issues — protection, structure, governance.
  • Evaluate competitive strategy — technology deployment, dominant design, entry.
  • Apply financial reasoning to pricing and budgeting decisions that keep a venture alive.
  • Communicate venture concepts through a written report, a pitch video, slides, and a presentation you have to defend.

Every one of these is assessed through the simulations and the team venture. There is no exam.
