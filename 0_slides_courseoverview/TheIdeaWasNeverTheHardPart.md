# The Idea Was Never the Hard Part: Why Execution Decides Everything

Every semester, somebody comes to office hours with an idea they will not describe until I promise not to steal it. I always promise, and I have never once been tempted, and the reason is not politeness. It is that **an idea is a free option and execution is the part where you pay**. The gap between the two is where entire companies are won and lost, and almost none of what happens in that gap is glamorous enough to fit in a pitch.

The clearest recent illustration ran in *The New York Times* in April. Erin Griffith reported on Medvi, a telehealth company selling GLP-1 weight-loss drugs, which did $401 million in sales in 2025 with **two employees** — the founder and his brother — while tracking toward $1.8 billion in 2026 (Griffith, 2026, The New York Times, via [Techmeme](https://www.techmeme.com/260402/p12)). The website, the customer service, the marketing, the ad creative, and the analytics were assembled with a handful of AI tools costing a few hundred dollars a month.

Now notice what was *not* rare in that story. The idea — sell GLP-1 medications direct to consumers online — was not a secret. It was one of the most obvious business ideas of the decade, and hundreds of people had it at the same time. What separated one of them from the rest was not insight. It was **the ordinary, sequential, deeply boring work of actually shipping**: getting the pharmacy relationships to function, getting the ads to convert, getting the payments to clear, getting the support queue answered on a Sunday.

The story has a second half worth teaching, and it is the reason I hand it to a class rather than a fan. Within days of the article, other outlets began surfacing allegations about the company's practices and its customers' experiences ([Techdirt](https://www.techdirt.com/2026/04/07/the-new-york-times-got-played-by-a-telehealth-scam-and-called-it-the-future-of-ai/), 2026). None of that is settled, and it is not my point. My point is narrower and it is about you: **a story about execution travels faster than execution does.** Revenue is a measurement of what customers paid. It is not, by itself, a measurement of what they received. Anyone can be described as executing. Far fewer are.

What makes this the right moment to teach execution is that the cost of the mechanical parts of it has genuinely collapsed. In a survey of roughly 81,000 people, Anthropic's economics team found that **"the mean productivity rating was 5.1, corresponding to 'substantially more productive'"**, and that the most common kind of gain was not speed but reach: **"the most common productivity enhancement is in scope, which was cited by 48% of users who explicitly mentioned productivity effects"** (Massenkoff & Huang, 2026, [Anthropic Research](https://www.anthropic.com/research/81k-economics)). *Scope* is the operative word. It means people are doing kinds of work they previously could not do at all — the founder who now writes the landing page, reads the contract, and builds the dashboard.

That should make you more ambitious and more nervous at once. If the tasks that used to separate the people with ideas from the people with companies are now available for a few hundred dollars a month, then **the barrier that used to protect your idea is gone, and it was protecting you from competitors as much as it was slowing you down.** Everyone in your section can now build the thing. The advantage moves to whoever decides faster, sequences better, and keeps going after the first version fails.

The same research is honest about the cost of that shift. One fifth of respondents **"voiced concern about economic displacement,"** and the concern tracked exposure closely: **"for every 10-percentage-point increase in exposure, perceived job threat increased by 1.3 percentage points"** (Massenkoff & Huang, 2026, [Anthropic Research](https://www.anthropic.com/research/81k-economics)). Whether that displacement is the end of work at all was argued directly at the Hopkins Forum in February, where Simon Johnson framed the era as **"an artificial intelligence designed deliberately to replicate and replace human intelligence,"** and Chris Hughes answered that **"technology did not herald the end of work"** (Johnson and Hughes, 2026, [Open to Debate](https://opentodebate.substack.com/p/the-hopkins-forum-will-ai-make-work)). Rumman Chowdhury added the line I would put on the syllabus: **"when AI makes work 'obsolete,' what that does not look like is a robot at your desk drinking your coffee."**

Which brings us to the question this whole course is built around, and which I cannot answer for you:

**If the execution of individual tasks is becoming cheap, what part of execution is still expensive — and can it be learned in a semester?**

My working answer is that what remains expensive is **judgment under incomplete information, exercised repeatedly, in public, with your own money at stake.** Which step of the value chain to own. Which cost to out-operate rather than outgrow. Which secret to keep and which to publish. What to charge for. How much of next year to promise away. Those are the eight decisions the rest of this semester walks through, and no tool makes them for you, because each one is a trade with no correct answer — only a defensible one.

So bring the idea. Everyone has one, and it is worth roughly what everyone else's is worth. Then spend fourteen weeks on the part that is actually scarce: **deciding, shipping, measuring, and being wrong early enough that it is still cheap.**
